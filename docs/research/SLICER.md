# Research report: slicer techniques for STL → STEP

**WORK IN PROGRESS: MANY FEATURES MAY BE BROKEN** (STL2STEP 0.4.0-wip)

This is the full text of a research report produced for STL2STEP in October 2026 by an AI research agent (Claude) from papers, slicer documentation and source (read for algorithms only), and a prototype measured in a 2-core cloud sandbox (not on the project's target laptops). It was **not independently verified by the author** and is published so others can check it, argue with it and build on it. A plain-English summary is in [README.md](README.md). Sandbox measurements and inferences are labelled as such. **Nothing from this report is built into the app yet.**

## Borrow slicer rules, not slicer layers

Yes, slicer techniques can turn an STL into a STEP solid, and STL2STEP already uses them: Loft and Extrude outline are a slicer that keeps the perimeters and throws away everything else. Your specific idea (a 0.001 mm "virtual nozzle" for outer walls, tops and bottoms, 1 mm for everything inside) has the right instinct and the wrong mechanism. A STEP solid has no interior, only a skin, so the internal saving you are after is automatic and total with no setting at all. Walls that run parallel to the slice axis are captured exactly at any layer spacing, so they need no fine treatment in Z. Tops, bottoms, domes and shallow slopes are where slicing stair-steps, and a finer nozzle does nothing for them because the error is in layer height, not in XY: a flat top is fixed by putting one slice exactly on it, and a dome needs either hundreds of layers or a proper curved face. Measured in a sandbox, a tolerance-driven slicer needs **5 slices for a prismatic bracket and 419 to 1,111 for a knob, blob or vase at 0.01 mm**, and the best slice-based prototype beat the existing engines on exactly one part (the knob: 2.9 s, 108 faces, 0.079 mm against Ladder's 13.4 s, 951 faces, 0.129 mm) while failing outright on every part with holes, on the vase and on the blob. So do not build a separate slicer mode. Fold three slicer ideas into what exists: sections at every flat level and every topology change (the slab decomposition already recommended for this project), in-between heights chosen by an error model instead of the area curve, and a per-island slab loft as a fallback rung in the Ladder engine. Every timing here comes from a 2-vCPU cloud Xeon, not an i3 or i5; the first measurement run was cut short, and multi-axis slicing was never run.

## A STEP solid is all perimeter, so the infill saving is already total

A slicer fills the inside of a part because a printer has to put plastic there. A STEP file does not. It stores a solid as a B-rep (boundary representation): a closed skin of faces, each one a true plane, cylinder, cone or spline surface, stitched along shared edges, plus a note of which side is material. In slicer terms it is a print with one perimeter of zero width, 0 % infill and no solid layers, vase mode taken to its limit. A cross-section of a mesh is the same thing one dimension down: closed outlines with nothing inside them. That settles the first half of the verdict. **"1 mm for all internal geometry" has nothing to act on**, because neither a section nor a STEP solid contains an interior to coarsen. The saving is already 100 %. One caution about words: if "internal geometry" means real inner surfaces (the bore of a hole, the inside of a hollow box), those are skin too. They are perimeters, and coarsening them to 1 mm would put a 1 mm error on a hole.

The idea is respectable where it comes from. "Accurate exterior, fast interior layered manufacturing" is the title of a 1997 paper in Rapid Prototyping Journal ([Sabourin et al. 1997](https://www.emerald.com/rpj/article-abstract/3/2/44/364208/Accurate-exterior-fast-interior-layered?redirectedFrom=fulltext); only the title was readable, the abstract was blocked), and region-based adaptive slicing splits a part into surface regions sliced thin and interior regions sliced thick so that users can "impose different surface finish requirements on different surfaces of the model" ([Mani, Kulkarni and Dutta 1999](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010448599000330); [ScienceDirect, Adaptive Slicing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/adaptive-slicing)). A printer gains from this because the interior costs print time. A CAD kernel gains nothing because the interior does not exist.

The second half of the verdict needs the two slicer knobs pulled apart. A slicer has an XY resolution (the nozzle and path resolution: how faithfully each layer's outline follows the model) and a Z resolution (layer height). A "virtual nozzle" is an XY knob. It controls how well each outline is traced, so it controls how good the **walls** are, because a wall parallel to the slice axis is nothing more than its outline pushed straight up. That part of the idea points the right way: walls live or die by outline accuracy. Spacing, though, is irrelevant to them. Slice a straight-sided box every 0.05 mm or every 50 mm and the wall is the same plane. **Tops and bottoms are the opposite case.** A face lying flat in the slice plane is invisible to every slice that does not land exactly on it, and its error is the distance to the nearest slice whatever the nozzle. So the idea spends its fine setting where Z resolution is not needed (walls) and turns the wrong knob where it is (tops and bottoms).

There is also no computation to save by coarsening anything. The figures that follow, and every figure marked "sandbox" in this report, come from a prototype run for this research and have no public source. One exact vector section cost **1.9 ms on the 2,958-triangle knob, 2.9 ms on the 9,944-triangle blob and 4.3 ms on the 24,146-triangle vase** with the app's own `profile2d.section`, so a thousand slices cost 1.9 to 4.3 s; trimesh's `section_multiplane` was about 25 % faster. Simplifying an outline took 0.1 to 0.5 ms whether the tolerance was 0.001 mm or 1 mm. Nozzle size changed the knob outline from 75 vertices to 9 and changed the cost by nothing worth measuring. The time goes on what happens after slicing: fitting curves to outlines (up to about 155 ms per loop when splines are needed) and building surfaces between slices. That is the real budget, and it is all on the outside of the part.

## Layer height sets the error: one slice per flat top, hundreds for a dome

Slicers have a one-line model for stair-stepping and it transfers to CAD unchanged. The cusp height (the depth of one stair-step) is the layer height times how much the surface faces the slice direction: **c = t × |n_z|**, where n_z is the Z part of the surface normal ([ScienceDirect, Adaptive Slicing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/adaptive-slicing)). A vertical wall has n_z = 0, so the error is zero at any layer height. A flat top has n_z = 1, so the error is the whole layer height unless a slice lands on it. Everything else sits in between: by that formula a 45° chamfer at 0.2 mm layers steps by 0.14 mm, and holding 0.01 mm on it needs 0.014 mm layers. Adaptive layer height is the same formula turned around, t = c_max / |n_z|, clamped between a minimum and a maximum layer; that is the classic Dolenc and Mäkelä rule every later scheme starts from ([Wasserfall et al. 2017](https://tams.informatik.uni-hamburg.de/publications/2017/Adaptive%20Slicing%20for%20the%20FDM%20Process%20Revisited.pdf)).

The slicers you know differ only in which flavour of that error they hold constant. PrusaSlicer's variable layer height limits "the cross-section area between the stepped surface and the source model" ([Prusa knowledge base](https://help.prusa3d.com/article/variable-layer-height-function_1750)) and returns an unlimited height for a vertical wall ([PrusaSlicer SlicingAdaptive.cpp](https://github.com/prusa3d/PrusaSlicer/blob/version_2.9.0/src/libslic3r/SlicingAdaptive.cpp)). Cura's Adaptive Layers keeps the horizontal step between neighbouring layers under a "topography size" of 0.2 mm, trying the thickest allowed layer first ([CuraEngine AdaptiveLayerHeights.cpp](https://github.com/Ultimaker/CuraEngine/blob/main/src/settings/AdaptiveLayerHeights.cpp); [Cura fdmprinter.def.json](https://github.com/Ultimaker/Cura/blob/main/resources/definitions/fdmprinter.def.json)). Two details in that code matter more than the formulas. PrusaSlicer measures the distance to the next horizontal facet so a layer boundary can be snapped onto a flat top, and it cuts a layer short when a steeper region starts part-way up it; Cura deliberately leaves flat faces out of the calculation to "prevent flat surfaces from influencing the algorithm". Both slicers treat a flat face as a level to land on, not as something to creep up on with thin layers. For a printer those are refinements. For CAD they are the whole method: **a flat face must be a section level, and a new region starting mid-slab must start a new slab.** One more difference: PrusaSlicer will not go below a 0.07 mm layer by default because a printer cannot ([PrusaSlicer PrintConfig.cpp](https://github.com/prusa3d/PrusaSlicer/blob/version_2.9.0/src/libslic3r/PrintConfig.cpp)). CAD has no such floor, which is exactly why the slice count explodes when you ask for CAD tolerances.

The sandbox ran a cusp-model picker over six meshes, with spacing clamped to 0.05–5 mm and flat faces taken as explicit levels on the bracket and the filleted block. The table gives the number of slices each setting asks for.

Read the bracket row first. Once its flat faces are levels it needs five slices at any tolerance, because all of its area is either horizontal (49 %) or vertical wall (51 %). That row is not a curiosity: about 43 % of the designs in one large CAD dataset are sketch-and-extrude only, though not all along a single axis ([Fusion 360 Gallery](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.02392)). Then read the rest. The knob has 30 % of its area sloped at 20–70° to the axis and 4 % nearly flat; the vase is 60 % sloped; the blob is 63 % sloped and 12 % nearly flat. They need hundreds of slices, and the picker puts them where the formula says. On the knob at 0.05 mm, the ten tenths of the height received 23, 0, 1, 1, 14, 33, 13, 18, 41 and 65 slices: almost none on the straight wall, most on the dome and fillets. On the filleted block, 235 of the 260 slices landed in the three tenths that hold the fillet. The vase and blob slope everywhere and were sliced nearly evenly. The vase's 1,111 sits at about the picker's 0.05 mm floor over its whole height, so read it as a lower bound for 0.01 mm, not as the true need. The app's own height picker (`pick_heights`, driven by the area curve) chooses 2 to 14 sections on the same parts, which is why Loft mode is fast and why it is loose on anything curved.

With uniform 0.2 mm layers the worst error on tops is 0.19–0.20 mm, which is the layer height: the last slice sits up to one layer short of the true top. **Snapping a slice to each flat level took the knob's tops from 0.191 mm to 0.075 mm** with no change of nozzle. Wall means are 0.0003 to 0.011 mm in the uniform, adaptive and slab rows, whether the wall was cut by 0.2 mm layers or by a handful of adaptive ones, which is the "walls are exact at any spacing" claim in numbers. (The wall bucket takes in surfaces up to 20° off vertical, which probably explains the 0.029 mm mean in the app's 8-section Loft row.) The wall maxima of 0.19 mm on the block and 0.127 mm on the bracket are not staircase error; the research notes attribute them to the solid ending a layer short and to the app's outline tolerance (auto, 0.2 % of part size), though no run isolated either cause. So your instinct that tops and bottoms need the special treatment is confirmed by measurement, and the treatment is a slice in the right place, not a finer one.

A loft softens the model for some surfaces and not others. Stacked extrusions inherit the cusp formula exactly. A loft between two outlines is exact wherever the surface runs straight between them (planes, cylinders, cones, drafted walls), so those need two sections, not three hundred; only curvature in the vertical direction costs sections. The slope-based picker does not know this, which is why the 12-triangle drafted box drew about 300 slices for six flat faces. The fix is to space lofted sections by curvature in the slicing direction, the approach of Kulkarni and Dutta's accurate slicing procedure ([ScienceDirect, Adaptive Slicing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/adaptive-slicing)), and slope only for stepped slabs. As plain arithmetic, not a measurement: a 15 mm fillet held to 0.01 mm by straight loft segments needs a section roughly every 4° of arc, about twenty in all, against the 379 the slope model asked for. A curvature picker was not built. And for domes and fillets the better answer is not more sections at all but a different kind of face. The existing engines already produce cylindrical and revolved faces, Ladder wrote the filleted block as 9 faces at 0.009 mm, and no stack of slices will match that. Slicer research reaches the same conclusion from its side; curved-layer slicing exists specifically to get rid of the staircase on shallow slopes ([CurviSlicer](https://www.academia.edu/112640212/CurviSlicer)).

## The real virtual nozzle is the contour tolerance, and 0.001 mm is finer than the mesh

The CAD equivalent of a nozzle is the tolerance used to turn a section's polyline into sketch curves. Slicers run the same two operations and publish their defaults. PrusaSlicer simplifies paths by Douglas-Peucker to a `gcode_resolution` of **0.0125 mm**, and Cura limits simplification to a maximum deviation of **0.025 mm** ([PrusaSlicer PrintConfig.cpp](https://github.com/prusa3d/PrusaSlicer/blob/version_2.9.0/src/libslic3r/PrintConfig.cpp); [Cura fdmprinter.def.json](https://github.com/Ultimaker/Cura/blob/main/resources/definitions/fdmprinter.def.json)). ArcWelder, the arc fitter that PrusaSlicer absorbed, defaults to 0.05 mm, meaning paths "can deviate by plus or minus 0.025mm" ([ArcWelderLib](https://github.com/FormerLurker/ArcWelderLib)). A 0.001 mm nozzle is 12 to 50 times finer than anything a production slicer uses.

It is also finer than your input. A CAD exporter puts mesh vertices on the true surface and lets the flat chords between them sag inside it by the export tolerance ([OCCT mesh guide](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/overview/html/occt_user_guides__mesh.html)), which as a rule of thumb is 0.01–0.1 mm. An outline traced at 0.001 mm therefore reproduces the facets, not the design. The sandbox shows it directly: a 99-vertex knob outline kept 75 vertices at 0.001 mm, 66 at 0.01 mm, 32 at 0.1 mm and 9 at 1 mm. At 0.001 mm you get a 75-sided polygon wall where the designer drew a circle. What recovers the circle is the fitter. The app's line/arc/ellipse/spline fitter (`profile2d._smooth_wire`) recognised all 20 knob loops as circles in 1.3 ms per loop. **The useful setting is the fit tolerance, and it should be read off the mesh's own chord error**; set it below that and the fitter rejects genuine arcs, set it at that and a faceted circle becomes one cylinder face. Your 0.001 mm is best read as "add no error of your own", which is the right goal with the wrong number.

The slicer arc-fitting recipe is worth copying as a method. PrusaSlicer first strips collinear points with Douglas-Peucker at 3 % of the tolerance, then grows an arc one point at a time, refits the centre by least squares, and rejects the arc if any vertex or the midpoint of any chord is off by more than the tolerance, so a chord sagging inside the arc is caught and not only its ends. It refuses radii above 600 mm ("it is better to fit with a line segment"), checks arc length against the original path within 5 %, and dropped ArcWelder's try-every-midpoint search because it "makes the time complexity of the arc fitting an ugly O(n^3)" ([PrusaSlicer ArcWelder.cpp](https://github.com/prusa3d/PrusaSlicer/blob/version_2.9.0/src/libslic3r/Geometry/ArcWelder.cpp); [ArcWelder.hpp](https://github.com/prusa3d/PrusaSlicer/blob/version_2.9.0/src/libslic3r/Geometry/ArcWelder.hpp)). The code is AGPL and cannot be copied, but the method is a page of NumPy.

One slicer habit should not be copied. Slicers simplify every layer on its own, and PrusaSlicer's own setting text admits that "because the G-code reduction is performed at each layer independently, visible artifacts may be produced." In a print that is a faint surface pattern. In a loft it is worse, because the kernel needs the outlines at both ends of a slab to have matching segments; OpenCASCADE's loft has a compatibility option that will "update wires to have same number of edges" for you, presumably by splitting edges where it sees fit ([OCCT BRepOffsetAPI_ThruSections](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_offset_a_p_i___thru_sections.html)). Fit lines and arcs first, then match segments between the two ends by type and position, and the loft gets clean input. Curve fitting is also where a many-slice approach quietly goes broke. Where outlines are not circles the app's fitter took 154 ms per loop on the vase and 159 ms on the blob. By simple arithmetic, 1,111 vase sections are about 5 s of slicing and at least three minutes of fitting, at one loop per section, before a single surface exists.

## Slicers get clean loops by rules you can re-implement but not copy

Every production slicer and the best academic one build layer outlines in the same three stages. First, triangles are bucketed by their Z range so each plane only visits the triangles that cross it. The optimal algorithm of Minetto and colleagues runs in time proportional to triangles plus planes plus output segments for uniform layers, and sliced a 16.7-million-triangle skull at 4,680 planes in 17.4 s where Slic3r took 24.5 s ([Minetto et al. 2017](https://www.inf.ufpr.br/murilo/public/CAD-slicing.pdf)). Second, each triangle is cut by each plane under a strict rule about who owns a vertex or edge lying exactly on the plane. PrusaSlicer's rule is that "the bottom most edge resp. vertex of a triangle is not owned by the triangle, but the top most edge resp. vertex is part of the triangle", and that an upward-facing horizontal face counts as on the plane while a downward-facing one does not ([PrusaSlicer TriangleMeshSlicer.cpp](https://github.com/prusa3d/PrusaSlicer/blob/version_2.9.0/src/libslic3r/TriangleMeshSlicer.cpp); [TriangleMeshSlicer.hpp](https://github.com/prusa3d/PrusaSlicer/blob/version_2.9.0/src/libslic3r/TriangleMeshSlicer.hpp)). Minetto avoids the question by rounding vertices to even multiples of a tiny unit and planes to odd multiples, so no vertex ever sits on a plane. Third, the cut segments are chained into loops by mesh connectivity: each segment end remembers which mesh edge it came from, and two segments join when they share an edge. No distances are compared at all.

Those two rules matter more for CAD than for printing. A printer slices at arbitrary heights and rarely hits a vertex ring. A CAD pipeline wants to slice exactly at flat-face levels, which is exactly where hundreds of vertices lie on the plane. Without an ownership rule the result is doubled edges or sliver loops; a random nudge off the level would bring back the top error the level was meant to remove. And for a watertight mesh, chaining by edge identity is exact with no tolerance. trimesh's multi-plane section returns the face behind each segment but not the edge ([trimesh intersections](https://trimesh.org/trimesh.intersections.html)); carrying the edge along as well is a small addition. That last step is an inference from reading the code, not something the prototype built.

What remains is the fallback ladder that lets slicers print broken meshes. When connectivity leaves open ends, PrusaSlicer joins them by exact endpoint match, then by gap closing up to 2 mm in two passes seeded from the longest chains, then unions all loops under a fill rule and applies a small closing offset so that "cracks smaller than 2x gap closing radius are being filled", with that radius defaulting to 0.049 mm ([PrusaSlicer TriangleMeshSlicer.cpp](https://github.com/prusa3d/PrusaSlicer/blob/version_2.9.0/src/libslic3r/TriangleMeshSlicer.cpp); [PrintConfig.cpp](https://github.com/prusa3d/PrusaSlicer/blob/version_2.9.0/src/libslic3r/PrintConfig.cpp)). Cura has the same shape: connectivity loops, small-gap joining, larger-gap stitching, and an "extensive stitching" option that is off by default and whose description warns it "can introduce a lot of processing time" ([CuraEngine slicer.cpp](https://github.com/Ultimaker/CuraEngine/blob/main/src/slicer.cpp); [Cura fdmprinter.def.json](https://github.com/Ultimaker/Cura/blob/main/resources/definitions/fdmprinter.def.json)).

The sandbox tested whether slicing by itself rescues a broken mesh, and it does not. With 1 % of triangles deleted, raw sections matched the intact mesh's section area within 2 % at **0 of 40 heights on the bracket, 0 of 40 on the plate and 10 of 40 on the blob**. The robustness lives in the gap-closing and union steps, which the prototype did not have. Run through the app, whose loader already fills such holes, both routes produced closed valid solids from the damaged files: the bracket came out at 8 faces and 0.007 mm either way; the plate at 25 faces and 0.210 mm from slices against 66 faces and 0.084 mm from Classic; the blob at 760 faces and **5.85 mm** from slices, because only its biggest island was lofted, against 24 faces and 0.058 mm from Classic. A slicer-style gap closer is therefore worth borrowing only for meshes the loader cannot repair, and nobody has measured how often gap closing bridges two outlines that were meant to be separate.

On licences the wall is clean. PrusaSlicer, CuraEngine and the ArcWelder code inside PrusaSlicer all carry AGPL-3 headers ([SlicingAdaptive.cpp](https://github.com/prusa3d/PrusaSlicer/blob/version_2.9.0/src/libslic3r/SlicingAdaptive.cpp); [AdaptiveLayerHeights.cpp](https://github.com/Ultimaker/CuraEngine/blob/main/src/settings/AdaptiveLayerHeights.cpp); [ArcWelder.cpp](https://github.com/prusa3d/PrusaSlicer/blob/version_2.9.0/src/libslic3r/Geometry/ArcWelder.cpp)), and their forks inherit it. None of that code can go into a PolyForm Noncommercial app. CGAL's mesh slicer should be treated as GPL until its header is checked ([CGAL Polygon_mesh_slicer](https://doc.cgal.org/latest/Polygon_mesh_processing/classCGAL_1_1Polygon__mesh__slicer.html)). The algorithms are free to re-implement from their descriptions, and the libraries that supply the 2D machinery are permissive: Clipper2 is Boost 1.0 ([Clipper2 licence](https://github.com/AngusJohnson/Clipper2/blob/main/LICENSE)), pyclipper is MIT ([pyclipper licence](https://github.com/fonttools/pyclipper/blob/main/LICENSE)), shapely is BSD 3-Clause ([shapely licence](https://github.com/shapely/shapely/blob/main/LICENSE.txt)) and trimesh is MIT ([trimesh licence](https://github.com/mikedh/trimesh/blob/main/LICENSE.md)). shapely's simplify, buffer and union cover everything the slicers use Clipper for. stlToSolid, the open converter whose loft method "slices the body along one axis every 0.2 mm" and lofts B-spline sections, is PolyForm Noncommercial: readable as a reference, not something to copy from ([stlToSolid](https://github.com/Crypto69/stlToSolid)).

## A loft takes one loop per layer, so slabs must split where holes and islands change

Here slicing and CAD part company for good. A slicer layer can hold any number of outlines: a ring, three islands, a plate with thirteen holes. OpenCASCADE's loft cannot. `BRepOffsetAPI_ThruSections` builds "a shell or a solid passing through a set of sections in a given sequence" from **one wire (one closed loop) per section**, and has no call for a section with two loops ([OCCT BRepOffsetAPI_ThruSections](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_offset_a_p_i___thru_sections.html)). In the sandbox every loft through a section with a hole failed with the kernel's "command not done" error. That one limit accounts for the plate and tray failing in every slice-based variant.

The problem is older than slicers. Rebuilding a surface from stacked contours is a 35-year-old research field with three named sub-problems: which points on one contour match which on the next, how to tile between them, and branching, which "occurs when one contour slice contains only one closed contour, while the next slice contains two or more closed contours" ([Braude et al. 2007](https://www.museth.org/Ken/Publications_files/Braude-etal_GM07.pdf)). The lesson of that literature is that branching is not interpolated, it is split out. Between two heights where the number and nesting of loops stay the same, a loft per loop is well defined. At a height where a hole starts, an island splits or a cavity closes, the slab has to end and a new one begin. The mathematical name for those heights is the critical levels of the Reeb graph of the height function ([Reeb graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reeb_graph)). In practice they only occur at mesh vertices, so they can be found before any slicing; that is an inference, as no published source was found that picks loft sections this way. The matching problem, at least, does not arise for a mesh: both ends of a slab come from the same triangles, so which point pairs with which is given.

The prototype found these events by bisection between slices. It found none on the knob, bracket, filleted block or plate (whose holes go right through), one on the tray (thirteen holes becoming one at z = 4.0), four on the vase (its inner cavity opening and closing) and four on the blob (islands splitting and merging). It then tried the obvious repair, a separate loft per loop with holes cut out by boolean, and **did not get it working on the plate or the tray**. That is a gap in the prototype, not proof the route is closed, but it is not proof it is open either. The cheaper route for the common case needs no loft at all: a slab whose two ends agree is an extrusion of one face with its holes already in it. The plate, with zero topology events, is one such slab. Only slabs that genuinely change shape need lofting, and only those need the one-loop-per-loft bookkeeping.

Two more kernel limits cap the many-thin-layers approach. The loft itself is fast (5 to 160 sections took 0.01 to 0.90 s) but it produced 30 to 3,341 faces and then raised a kernel error past about 160 sections. And loop count staying constant does not mean nothing happened: an overhang or undercut can start between two sections without changing the count. Comparing the area between consecutive outlines and splitting where it jumps is the proposed check, and it is untested.

## The prototype won on one part and failed on every part with holes

The main sandbox table compares four slice-based variants with the two existing engines. "Uniform" is 0.2 mm layers lofted; "adaptive" is the cusp picker at 0.3 mm, lofted; "app Loft" is the current mode with up to 15 sections; "slab" splits at flat levels and topology changes and lofts each island with adaptive heights. Cells give time, face count and maximum deviation. Times include about 2 s of process start-up, and every result that completed was a closed valid solid.

| Mesh | Uniform 0.2 mm loft | Adaptive 0.3 mm loft | App Loft | Slab | Classic | Ladder |

The knob is the one clear win: **four to six times faster than either engine, a ninth of the faces, and a better deviation**. Its time split shows why. Of 2.9 s, about 2 s is start-up, sections took 0.22 s, the topology search 0.15 s, the loft 0.06 s, fix and unify 0.11 s, STEP write and read-back 0.18 s and the deviation check 0.35 s. There is no retry loop, no plane search and no patch fitting. The knob is also a part that suits slicing unusually well: a turned body with no holes and no topology events, whose outlines are all circles.

Everywhere else the existing engines win or the slice route does not finish. On the bracket, slicing's best case, the slab variant ties Classic on time and is **18 times less accurate** (0.129 mm against 0.007 mm). The notes blame the outline tolerance and argue that a prismatic part sliced at its levels with a tight tolerance is exact in principle; that is credible and no run demonstrated it. On the filleted block the slab result is about 20 times worse than Ladder, because a stack of sections is competing with one true cylinder face. The drafted box is the bluntest row: twelve triangles, six planes, 2.1 s in either engine with zero error, against 27 s for uniform layers and a 400 s timeout for adaptive ones. The vase lofts through hundreds of spline sections never finished, while Ladder wrote 14 faces in 10.6 s. The blob either timed out or lofted only its biggest island.

One property of slicing does stand out, and it is the last row. A section through a subdivided mesh is the same curve as a section through the original, so the slice variants returned almost the same result on the subdivided knob (107 faces against 109, same deviation) as on the plain one. Classic on the same file collapsed into its faceted fallback after 88.5 s. Slicing is blind to mesh density in a way the face-based detectors are not. That is a real robustness property, and it argues for using sections as a cross-check even where they are not used to build the solid.

## What to build, in order, and what to leave alone

The ranking weighs evidence against effort for one developer. Nothing here is a new mode. Extrude outline is slab decomposition with one slab, Loft is slab decomposition with sections that vary, and the first three items are that same code made stricter. All of it stays behind the existing rule that a result is accepted only if it matches the full mesh within tolerance.

| Rank | Build | Where it goes | Why | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sections exactly on every flat-face level (with the slicer's on-plane ownership rule) and a slab split at every topology change. Item 1 is the one that turns the idea in this report into something correct: your "fine on tops and bottoms" becomes "a section on every top and bottom", and "coarse inside" becomes "nothing inside". Items 2 and 3 are what stop it being slow and loose. Item 4 is the only place the measurements say a slice-based result should replace what an engine produces today, and it rests on a single knob.

## Honesty ledger

**Published.** The cusp-height model and the adaptive-thickness rule built on it; the existence of exterior-fine, interior-coarse slicing in the printing literature; how PrusaSlicer and Cura choose adaptive layers, snap to or exclude flat faces, own on-plane edges, chain by connectivity, close gaps, union and simplify; the slicer tolerance defaults; the arc-fitting method; Minetto's optimal slicing algorithm and its timings; the branching problem in contour reconstruction; the one-wire-per-section loft interface; and every licence quoted. The slicer source was read at PrusaSlicer's 2.9.0 tag and CuraEngine's main branch. Weak spots: CuraEngine's slicer file was read through an automated summary, so its gap thresholds are left out here; the Sabourin 1997 abstract was blocked and only its title is used; Dolenc and Mäkelä's and Kulkarni and Dutta's formulas are quoted through secondary sources; Meyers 1992 is cited through Braude 2007; ArcWelder's AGPL status comes from the header of PrusaSlicer's port, not from its own repository; CGAL's package licence was not confirmed. No published accuracy study of slicer arc fitting was found, and no published source applies critical levels to choosing loft sections.

**Sandbox, not an i3 or i5.** Every time, slice count, face count and deviation in this report comes from a 2-vCPU cloud Xeon, one run each, in Python with cadquery-ocp, trimesh and shapely, with the repository read-only, over nine meshes of 12 to 24,146 triangles. Nothing was run on a 7th-generation i3, an i5, Windows or macOS. The first measurement run was cut off by a usage limit after the cost, slice-count and build experiments; the walls-versus-tops and broken-mesh experiments were run afterwards in a second pass, and the topology-event and time-split figures come from log lines inside the build experiment, not from experiments of their own. **Multi-axis slicing with boolean intersection was not run.** No slice-based run used a tight outline tolerance, so "exact on prismatic parts" has no number behind it. Holes by separate lofts and a boolean cut were attempted and did not complete. The taper's slab run hit an import error in the prototype. The broken-mesh test only deleted 1 % of triangles; large holes, self-intersections and overlapping shells were not tried. In that test a slice path produced a plate that every slice variant in the build experiment failed on, and its times appear to leave out start-up; the notes do not say which slice path it used, so the two experiments should not be compared row for row. The prototype scripts were not kept with the repository.

**Inference.** That 0.001 mm is finer than a typical exported mesh's chord error; that exact chaining by edge identity can be added to trimesh sections; that topology events occur only at mesh vertices and can be found without bisection; curvature-based spacing for lofts and the twenty-section fillet arithmetic; the three-minute fitting estimate for the vase; the reading of the vase's 1,111 as a clamp floor; the attribution of the bracket's 0.129 mm to outline tolerance; the area-jump test for overhangs; the gap-closer recipe; multi-axis slicing as a way to recover tops; and the ranking itself. One number deserves a flag: the taper's adaptive counts are higher than the cusp formula gives for a wall one or two degrees off vertical, so that row likely reflects how the prototype picker treated the end faces and should be read as an upper figure.

## Conclusion

The idea had its cost model upside down, and that is the useful thing to take from it. In a slicer the inside is where the time goes and the outline is nearly free. In a CAD kernel the inside does not exist, cutting the outline costs milliseconds, and the whole bill is for what gets built between one outline and the next. A scheme that economises on the interior saves nothing, and a scheme that buys accuracy with more and thinner layers pays the highest price there is. What slicers know that the app's Loft mode does not is where a layer boundary has to fall: on every flat face, and at every height where the count of loops changes. Those two rules are worth more than any resolution setting, and they are the same slab decomposition the earlier research arrived at from the CAD side.

The knob result changes one thing going forward. It suggests there is a cheap middle rung between "an exact revolve was found" and "fit patches", namely slice the island, loft it, check it, in three seconds. Whether that holds beyond one knob is the first thing to measure, along with a bracket sliced at its levels with a tight outline tolerance and a plate built as one extruded face with its holes. Those three runs on your own i3 would settle more than everything in the sandbox table.
