# Claim exact surfaces before fitting freeform

STL2STEP becomes both cheaper and more accurate by reordering its work rather than by fitting harder: claim every surface that has an exact description first, on the original full-resolution vertices, and hand only the leftover to a freeform stage rebuilt around four-sided patches. Your own measurements show that the cost sits in fallbacks, not in core fitting: **67% of the 41-minute ghost run is the `BRepFill_Filling` fallback**, and with only 331 NURBS faces among 40,484, nearly every face in that file is a fallback triangle. The half-second line-geometry test shows that **about 83% of the ghost's area** (72% in two surfaces of revolution, roughly 11% in cylindrical tunnels) can become a handful of exact faces before any spline is fitted. For the remainder, the literature and every commercial auto-surfacer converge on one design: a network of four-sided patches with shared, smoothed boundary curves, each flattened by a harmonic map so that it cannot fold, and each written as an untrimmed face. That removes the trimming step where about 150 of your patches fail validity today. Hole and flat accuracy of 0.05 mm comes from growing regions over triangle adjacency, refitting on vertices (which lie on the true surface in CAD-exported STL) with a curvature-parametrised least-squares fit, and snapping axes and radii to shared values. Every component is CPU-only and pip-installable; machine-learning methods need GPUs, are trained only on CAD benchmarks and mostly carry licences that clash with yours, so they contribute ideas rather than code. The remaining minutes come out by batching verification, building faces on shared edges instead of sewing, and using processes rather than threads, because the OCP binding for OpenCASCADE does not release Python's global lock.

## Fallbacks, not fitting, set the 41 minutes and 40,484 faces

Three of your measurements locate the problem. Two thirds of the 41-minute ghost run goes to `BRepFill_Filling`, a "plate" solver that deforms a surface until it passes through points sampled along the region's boundary. OCCT's defaults sample **15 points per edge, iterate twice with more points each round, and then approximate the result with B-splines of up to degree 8 in up to 9 segments** ([BRepFill_Filling reference](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_fill___filling.html)). Second, 331 NURBS faces inside a 40,484-face result means roughly 40,000 faces are single fallback triangles, so the face count and the 107 MB file measure how much surface failed, not how much succeeded. That hurts downstream: the author of mesh2step notes that one STEP face per surviving triangle "reads back slowly" in other tools ([mesh2step](https://github.com/tommasobbianchi/mesh2step)), and Rhino users report that 40,000–68,000 separate NURBS faces "can easily freeze" the program ([McNeel forum](https://discourse.mcneel.com/t/what-is-tonurb-s-limit/219422)). Third, Guesstimate mode proves the fits themselves are cheap at 4.3 minutes, yet it still leaves 513 triangle regions, so decimation alone does not touch the failure rate.

The improvement therefore has three levers, in this order. Shrink the area that ever reaches freeform fitting, because exact surfaces are both cheaper and more accurate than any spline. Build freeform faces that cannot fail in the ways today's trimmed patches fail. Make whatever fallback remains cost milliseconds instead of seconds. The sections below follow the pipeline in the order the data flows; the plan at the end reorders the work by payoff per unit of effort.

## Exact holes and flats come from adjacency, vertex refits and snapping

### Region growing over neighbouring triangles beats point-cloud RANSAC on exported meshes

RANSAC guesses a shape from a few random points, counts how many points agree, and keeps the best guess. The standard fast version, Schnabel's "Efficient RANSAC", owes its speed to two tricks: it draws each guess's points from one small spatial cell, and it scores guesses on a small random subset before committing. Together they cut the paper's 542,000-point test **from 272 s to 12.3 s** ([Schnabel et al. 2007](https://www.hinkali.com/Education/PointCloud.pdf)). Both tricks are worth porting into your existing detector if you keep it as a fallback for scan-like input. But RANSAC is a point-cloud method. It ignores which triangles touch, accepts anything inside a tolerance band, and in the authors' own oil-pump test left 15,000 points unclassified, mostly on "blending patches", meaning fillets ([Schnabel et al. 2007](https://www.hinkali.com/Education/PointCloud.pdf)). On the ABCParts CAD benchmark it reaches 70.1% segmentation overlap against 85.2% for a learned segmenter ([HPNet, ICCV 2021](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2021/papers/Yan_HPNet_Deep_Primitive_Segmentation_Using_Hybrid_Representations_ICCV_2021_paper.pdf)).

An STL exported from CAD carries the information RANSAC discards. Region growing starts at a seed triangle and absorbs neighbours that fit the same surface. It never merges two separate coaxial holes and never leaks across a sharp edge, provided that edges whose dihedral angle (the angle between two neighbouring triangles) exceeds a threshold act as walls. The quadric-fitting variant of variational shape approximation uses 30° walls and shows why they matter: segmentation took **3–25 s on 13,000–38,000-face meshes with feature pre-splitting, but more than an hour on a 100,000-face model without it** ([Yan et al.](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/Variational-mesh-segmentation-via-quadric-surface-fitting.pdf)). No library supplies this ready-made within your licence. CGAL's region growing on meshes ships a plane fit only ([CGAL Shape Detection manual](https://doc.cgal.org/latest/Shape_detection/index.html)), most CGAL algorithm packages are GPL ([CGAL licence](https://www.cgal.org/license.html)), and Attene's hierarchical primitive fitter is GPL and covers only planes, spheres and cylinders ([EfPiSoft](https://efpisoft.sourceforge.net/)). It is small to write, though. For planes it collapses into one compiled call: keep only the adjacency links whose two triangles agree in normal direction and plane offset, then label the connected pieces with `scipy.sparse.csgraph.connected_components` ([SciPy](https://docs.scipy.org/doc/scipy/reference/generated/scipy.sparse.csgraph.connected_components.html)). For curved regions, classify each wall-bounded region with the 6×6 line-geometry matrix you have already tested (next section): three near-zero eigenvalues mean plane or sphere, two mean a right circular cylinder, and the eigenvector supplies the axis directly, which replaces random hypothesis sampling altogether ([Pottmann et al. 2004](https://www.geometrie.tuwien.ac.at/geom/ig/papers/eccv04_lg.pdf)).

### A curvature-parametrised refit on vertices reaches 0.05 mm

Detection only labels a region; exactness comes from the refit. Lukács, Marshall and Martin give least-squares formulations that minimise true geometric distance and parametrise each shape by curvature instead of radius (sphere 4 parameters, cylinder 5, cone 6, torus 7), so a fit slides gracefully toward the simpler shape instead of diverging: a torus degenerates into a sphere as its major radius vanishes and into a cone as its minor radius grows. Solved with Levenberg–Marquardt, a damped least-squares iteration, the fits recovered simulated parts **to at least four significant digits** ([Lukács et al. 1998](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/48135/1/faithful.pdf)). This maps onto `scipy.optimize.least_squares`, started from the axis the eigenvector gives; the research found no maintained Python package that implements the cone and torus cases, so this is code to write. Stability needs attention: in the SHREC 2022 primitive-fitting contest, the entries that used plain least-squares post-fitting showed outliers from numerical instability, and tori were the hardest primitive for all six methods ([SHREC 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.07636)). Scale each region to a unit box, bound the curvature parameters, and keep the damping.

Which points you fit matters as much as how. CAD meshers put vertices on the exact surface and limit the facets by a linear deflection (the largest chord-to-surface gap) and an angular deflection; OCCT's guide gives 12–20° as the usual angular range ([OCCT mesh guide](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/overview/html/occt_user_guides__mesh.html)), and SolidWorks guidance gives 0.03–0.3 mm chord height with a 10° default angle ([CATI](https://cati.com/blog/2015/05/stl-output-settings-for-cad)). A 10° step leaves a sag of R(1 − cos 5°), which is **0.019 mm on a 5 mm-radius hole and 0.19 mm on a 50 mm-radius cylinder** unless the chord limit bites first. Triangle centroids therefore sit up to several times your 0.05 mm target inside the true surface while the vertices sit on it. Fit to vertices only. Use the centroid residuals, which for a correct primitive are one-sided and bounded by the sag, as the acceptance test, and set each region's tolerance from its own measured sag instead of a global constant. That acceptance test is an inference from the mesher documentation, not a published method, so validate it on the bracket and tray first. The same reasoning fixes the stage order: detect and refit primitives on the original mesh, before decimation, because quadric decimation moves vertices to error-minimising positions that no longer lie on the true surface.

Two further rules convert good fits into few, exact faces. Choose the model the way Point2CAD does, fitting each candidate type and keeping "the simplest model with the lowest reconstruction error" ([Point2CAD, CVPR 2024](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Liu_Point2CAD_Reverse_Engineering_CAD_Models_from_3D_Point_Clouds_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf)). Then snap: MeshToFeatures, the one open-source project doing feature-level reverse engineering of prismatic STL, unifies near-parallel directions to shared axes, merges coaxial cylinders and rounds values, with a snapping tolerance of about 0.1% of the part diagonal ([MeshToFeatures](https://github.com/MasoudMiM/MeshToFeatures)). Fragments of one plane or one cylinder that end up with identical parameters can then be merged into a single face. The tray's 635–1,927 faces against an original 119 most plausibly come from such fragments plus unrecognised fillets; that is a hypothesis to confirm by counting output faces per surface type. For hole rims, the namesake MIT-licensed project boromyr/STL2STEP "reconstructs cylindrical holes with exact circular edges" and verifies every local replacement immediately (solid still closed, faces valid, area and volume consistent with the mesh), a discipline worth copying outright ([boromyr/STL2STEP](https://github.com/boromyr/STL2STEP)).

### Fillets split into cylinders, tori and spheres by their spine

A constant-radius fillet is the surface a rolling ball leaves between two faces: a cylinder along a straight edge, a torus along a circular edge, a sphere at a corner. Recognition work on CAD models identifies blend faces as smooth strips that are tangent to their neighbours and small in area beside them ([Venkataraman et al. 2004](https://www.cad-journal.net/files/vol_1/CAD_1%281-4%29_2004_421-428.pdf)), and a 2022 mesh method segments them by requiring the ball to stay tangent to both adjacent surfaces ([Zhang et al.](https://doi.org/10.1109/tvcg.2020.3045450)). No open implementation of that method was found, and DeFillet, the nearest open tool, is AGPL-licensed ([DeFillet](https://github.com/xiaowuga/DeFillet)). The practical recipe follows from the definition. Detect the primary planes and cylinders first; treat the leftover strips tangent to two of them as blend candidates; take the fillet radius from the strip's constant curvature; then classify each piece by its spine, the path of the ball's centre. A straight spine is a cylinder, a circular spine is a torus whose major radius equals the spine's radius, and a point is a sphere. Testing whether a curve is straight or circular is far more robust than a seven-parameter torus search. Forcing all pieces of one fillet chain to share a single radius also removes the classic false positive in which a short torus segment is accepted as a corner sphere. Fusion draws the same line commercially: its fast face-group mode "may miss more complex geometry like filleted edges", while its slower accurate mode handles them ([Autodesk](https://www.autodesk.com/products/fusion-360/blog/generate-face-groups-fusion/)).

## One 6×6 eigenproblem lets a roughing stage claim 83% of the ghost

### The eigenvalues name the motion and the eigenvector gives the axis

A surface that some rigid motion slides along itself is cheap to recognise: a rotation gives a surface of revolution, a translation an extrusion, a screw motion a helix. For each triangle with centroid x, unit normal n and area w, form the six numbers u = (x × n, n), add up A = Σ w·u·uᵀ, and take the eigenvalues of that 6×6 matrix. The smallest eigenvalue measures how well one motion fits, and its eigenvector (c, c̄) is the motion: the pitch is p = (c·c̄)/‖c‖², the axis direction is c, and the axis position follows from c̄ − p·c ([Pottmann et al. 2004](https://www.geometrie.tuwien.ac.at/geom/ig/papers/eccv04_lg.pdf)). Bartoň, Pottmann and Wallner state the same condition as "the surface velocity is perpendicular to every normal" and add a reweighting loop, with weights 1/(1 + 400·f²) on a per-vertex misfit f, that isolates the part of a mesh obeying the motion without needing connectivity; they also advise removing planes and rotational surfaces before looking for anything more general ([Bartoň et al. 2014](https://www.geometrie.tuwien.ac.at/geom/ig/publications/2014/sweep2014/sweep2014.pdf)). The cost is one pass over the triangles and a constant-size eigen-solve, which matches the half second you measured.

| Near-zero eigenvalues | Deciding test | Surface | OCCT construction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | all normals parallel, or all normal lines through one point | plane, or sphere | analytic plane or sphere face |
| 2 | none needed | right circular cylinder | analytic cylinder face |
| 1 | pitch small against the region's size | surface of revolution (a cone is the straight-profile case) | `BRepPrimAPI_MakeRevol` of a fitted profile |
| 1 | rotation part near zero, pitch very large | extrusion | `BRepPrimAPI_MakePrism` of a fitted outline |
| 1 | finite pitch | helical surface | defer |
| 0 | none | freeform | patch stage |

Three implementation details decide whether this works per region and not only on the whole model. Centre each region and divide by its radius before building A, because the two halves of u have different units and the eigenvalue thresholds otherwise depend on model size. Weight by triangle area, because triangle density is not sampling density. And set the acceptance threshold from the data: the papers give only "small" (one follow-up quotes a single value of 0.02, [Yi et al. 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010448514000724)), so compare the root-mean-square tangency error with the normal noise estimated from neighbouring triangles. Several coaxial features share one motion and need no separation at this stage; they separate later as disjoint branches of the profile. Features on different axes are grown as separate regions by absorbing neighbours whose normals satisfy the current motion.

### Profiles reuse the Smooth-sketch fitter, and OCCT builds native faces from them

The linear estimate of the axis should be polished. Lai and Ueng showed that fixing the axis first and fitting the profile second accumulates error, and that solving both together held a vase with 0.1 mm of added noise to **0.173 mm maximum deviation** ([Lai and Ueng 2000](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0166361599000433)); on a clean export, where the only error is facet sag, the result should be limited by the mesh itself. To get the profile, map every vertex to (r, z), its distance from and position along the axis. A full revolution stacks all its vertices into one thin 2D band, so the profile is sampled far more densely than any single cross-section. Do not sort by z, which fails on undercuts; cut the mesh with a few half-planes through the axis instead, since each cut is already an ordered polyline, and average them. Classical reverse engineering then fits the profile as "smoothly connected straight line segments and circular arcs" ([Benkő et al. 2001](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010448501001002)), with splines for what is left and corners kept as hard breakpoints. That is the job your Smooth sketch option already does for extruded outlines, so the roughing stage is mostly new plumbing around an existing fitter.

In OCCT, `BRepPrimAPI_MakeRevol` lifts an edge to a face, a wire to a shell and a face to a solid, with an optional angle for partial revolutions ([MakeRevol reference](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_prim_a_p_i___make_revol.html)). Prefer it to hand-building a revolved surface, because it creates the seam and the degenerate edges at the axis for you, and because profile lines and arcs come out as native cylinders, cones, spheres and tori that other CAD programs treat as analytic. One trap is documented: a profile vertex within 1e-7 of the axis but not on it produces a solid that fails checking with a **volume 13–23% too small**, so snap such points exactly onto the axis and never let the profile cross it ([OCCT issue #1566](https://github.com/Open-Cascade-SAS/OCCT/issues/1566)).

Trimming is where exactness is won or lost. Where two recognised features meet, such as the ghost's body and its eye tunnels, build both as slightly oversized solids and let a boolean cut compute the exact intersection edge. Where a revolved face borders freeform surface there is no second solid, and OCCT's rule applies: on a non-planar surface, `BRepBuilderAPI_MakeFace` needs a pcurve (the edge redrawn in the surface's own 2D coordinates) for every edge, "otherwise the wrong shape will be created" ([MakeFace reference](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_builder_a_p_i___make_face.html)). A revolved surface makes that easy, because every boundary vertex converts to (angle, position along profile) in closed form; fit a 2D curve through those points and build the edge from it, so the 2D and 3D curves agree by construction. Place the seam, the line where angle 0 meets 360°, where no boundary crosses it. This junction between exact faces and patches is the least documented part of the whole design, and forum reports on OCCT's projection tools are mixed ([OCCT forum](https://dev.opencascade.org/content/brepprojprojection-wire-which-already-lying-face-does-not-work)), so budget time for it.

### Extrusions and pipes are worth claiming; general sweeps are not

An extrusion is the translation case: all wall normals are perpendicular to one direction, found as the smallest eigenvector of the 3×3 matrix Σ w·n·nᵀ, a test even cheaper than the 6×6 one. Project the region onto the plane across that direction, fit the outline with the same sketch fitter, take the extent from the range along the direction, and snap it to cap planes where they exist. This is the "barrel and base" decomposition that Point2Cyl formalises ([Point2Cyl](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09329)). It generalises your Extrude outline mode from whole parts to sub-regions, and stepped parts become several extrusions sharing a direction. Pipes and cables are the one curved sweep that stays cheap: move each vertex inward along its normal by the local radius of curvature and the surface collapses onto its centre line, the "shrinking" method ([Lee, shrinking method](https://www.academia.edu/735236/Reconstruction_of_Pipe_and_Canal_Surfaces_Using_Shrinking_Method)). A straight centre line is a cylinder, a circular one a torus, and only otherwise is `BRepOffsetAPI_MakePipeShell` needed ([MakePipeShell reference](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_offset_a_p_i___make_pipe_shell.html)). General freeform sweeps are a different matter. The reference method needs an exhaustive search over cutting planes, took **15–30 minutes on a 414,000-vertex model**, relies on hand-set thresholds and does not support non-planar profiles ([Bartoň et al. 2014](https://www.geometrie.tuwien.ac.at/geom/ig/publications/2014/sweep2014/sweep2014.pdf)). Leave those regions to the patch stage.

## Decimate only the leftover, verify the bound, and find edges by curvature

### Quadric decimation is fast but promises nothing, so measure after every run

The quadric error metric inside pyfqmr scores an edge collapse by summed squared distances to the planes of the original triangles, which is not a limit on the maximum distance ([CGAL simplification manual](https://doc.cgal.org/latest/Surface_mesh_simplification/index.html)). Methods with a true two-sided guarantee keep the simplified mesh inside an envelope around the original ([Cohen et al. 1996](https://gamma.cs.unc.edu/ENVELOPES/ENVELOPES.pdf)); the practical modern version is CGAL's polyhedral envelope filter, which is C++ and falls under CGAL's licensing ([CGAL simplification manual](https://doc.cgal.org/latest/Surface_mesh_simplification/index.html)). The pip-level equivalent is certification after the fact: decimate, measure the distance in both directions against the full mesh, and retry with a gentler target when the bound is exceeded. Both directions are needed because the standard sampling tools measure one way only and must be run twice ([MeshLab Stuff](http://meshlabstuff.blogspot.com/2010/01/measuring-difference-between-two-meshes.html)); fine-to-coarse catches lost detail while coarse-to-fine catches bridged gaps. Sample triangle interiors as well as vertices; vertex-only sampling misses the peak between samples.

Watertightness needs its own guard. The engine behind pyfqmr uses no priority queue: it sweeps all edges and collapses any whose error is under a rising threshold, its only safety test rejects collapses that flip a triangle, and its author says it works best on "watertight volumes without thin sections" ([Fast-Quadric-Mesh-Simplification](https://github.com/sp4cerat/Fast-Quadric-Mesh-Simplification)). That explains holes appearing at aggressive targets. libigl's `decimate` and `qslim` run a collapse validity test and also return, for every output face and vertex, the input element it came from ([libigl Python docs](https://libigl.github.io/libigl-python-bindings/igl_docs/)); Open3D replaced its decimator after a report that it "leaves holes" ([Open3D issue #4083](https://github.com/isl-org/Open3D/issues/4083)). Speed favours the fast engine: on one benchmark model it took 2.05–2.75 s against about 9.5 s for VTK's queue-based decimator ([fast-simplification](https://pyvista.github.io/fast-simplification/)). Keep the fast engine as the default, check `is_watertight` after every run, and fall back to `igl.qslim` on failure.

### Decimate by region and keep the ancestry

Your current pass reaches 40,000 triangles within 0.0096 mm, about a fifth of the 0.05 mm target. A quarter of the tolerance is a sensible budget, leaving the rest to fitting error. But once primitives and revolved surfaces are claimed on the full mesh, only organic regions remain, and they are held to 0.3 mm, so their decimation budget rises to roughly 0.075 mm and their triangle count falls in exactly the regions that go on to the expensive stage. This requires the borders of each region to stay fixed. pyfqmr offers only `preserve_border`, which protects open borders ([pyfqmr](https://github.com/seung-lab/pyfqmr-Fast-Quadric-Mesh-Reduction)), and that is enough if you cut the organic regions out and decimate them separately. The meshoptimizer package adds a per-vertex lock but ships on PyPI as source only, so it needs a compiler on the user's machine ([meshoptimizer on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/meshoptimizer/)). Keep the mapping between resolutions: the `fast-simplification` package returns the collapse list and an original-to-simplified vertex map ([fast-simplification](https://pyvista.github.io/fast-simplification/)), and because every fine vertex has exactly one coarse ancestor ([Hoppe 1996](https://hhoppe.com/pm.pdf)), a region labelled on the coarse mesh transfers to the full mesh by array lookup. Fit on the coarse mesh, verify each face against the full-resolution vertices of its region, and refine only the regions that fail.

### Rounded edges need curvature, not dihedral angles

Mesh-edge boundary chains work when a sharp edge is one triangle wide. On sculpted or scanned meshes a real edge is rounded over three to five triangles, each turning 20–30°, so no single mesh edge crosses a 30° threshold and a lower threshold fires on noise. The established answer is crest lines: the curves along which the larger principal curvature peaks, meaning the centre line of the rounded band, filtered by curvature summed along each line so that noise drops out. Yoshizawa and colleagues computed curvature and its derivatives in 9 s and the lines in 4 s on a model of more than 200,000 triangles on a 1.7 GHz Pentium 4, and found the result depends "only slightly on the quality of the mesh" ([Yoshizawa et al. 2005](https://www2.riken.jp/brict/Yoshizawa/Papers/spm05ybs.pdf)). Their code is licensed for research and education only ([crest-line code](https://www2.riken.jp/brict/Yoshizawa/Research/Crest.html)), so reimplement from the paper, or use Hildebrandt's simpler variant built on discrete operators ([Hildebrandt et al. 2005](https://ddg.math.uni-goettingen.de/pub/feature.pdf)). The easiest first version is normal voting: collect the triangle normals around each vertex into a 3×3 matrix, whose eigenvalues separate flat, edge and corner vertices however many triangles the bend spans ([Kim et al. 2009](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010448508002297)). libigl supplies principal curvatures as a pip-installable starting point ([libigl Python docs](https://libigl.github.io/libigl-python-bindings/igl_docs/)). Once the surfaces on both sides are fitted, move each feature line onto their intersection, which is where a CAD edge belongs.

Repair before all of this deserves caution. pymeshfix is GPL-3.0, returns "a single closed solid object" by design, and its documentation warns it may fail on tessellated CAD ([pymeshfix](https://github.com/pyvista/pymeshfix)). ManifoldPlus succeeded on all 4,899 models of a test set where MeshFix failed on 2,416, but it re-meshes the surface and is a C++ tool ([ManifoldPlus](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2005.11621)). Since STL stores no connectivity, merging duplicate vertices is the first and usually sufficient step; any stronger repair should be compared against the input and refused when it moves the surface beyond tolerance.

## Four-sided harmonic patches remove trimming, folding and the plate solver

### The layout causes the failures

Geodesic-Voronoi regions are many-sided with zig-zag borders; the paper behind the related shape-approximation method itself notes that region boundaries are jagged without post-processing ([Cohen-Steiner et al.](https://www.geometry.caltech.edu/pubs/CAD04.pdf)). A B-spline surface is a rectangular grid of control points, so any region that is not four-sided must be cut out of a larger surface by trimming, and trimming is where your faces fail. The alternative has been stable for thirty years. Eck and Hoppe partition the mesh into four-sided regions, flatten each with a harmonic map and fit one bicubic patch per region, refining only where error exceeds tolerance; their 69,473-face bunny needed 153 patches ([Eck and Hoppe 1996](https://hhoppe.com/bspline.pdf)). Mosbach and colleagues fit one B-spline per cell of a quad mesh with at least 5×5 control points ([Mosbach et al. 2020](https://web.cs.ucdavis.edu/~hamann/MosbachSchladitzHamannHagenComputingAndInformationScienceInEngineeringPaperAsAcceptedFinal08072020.pdf)), Autodesk's patented large-mesh fitter tiles a parametrisation into equal patches ([US10296664](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10296664)), and Geomagic Design X's automatic surfacing runs contours, then a patch network, then repair, then fitting ([Laser Design](https://www.laserdesign.com/4-easy-steps-to-nurbs-surface-modeling-in-geomagic-design-x/)).

A quad layout is obtainable in pure pip. `pynanoinstantmeshes` wraps the Instant Meshes remesher under a BSD licence; `remesh(verts, faces, face_count=N, rosy=4, posy=4, creaseAngle=…, align_to_boundaries=True)` returns a quad mesh whose edges follow creases and open borders ([PyNanoInstantMeshes](https://github.com/vork/PyNanoInstantMeshes)). Its wheels cover CPython 3.8–3.12 on Linux, Windows and Apple-Silicon macOS ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/pynanoinstantmeshes/)), while the current OCCT wheels cover Python 3.11–3.14 ([cadquery-ocp on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/cadquery-ocp/)), so Python 3.11 or 3.12 is the common ground, and Intel Macs need checking. Treat each coarse quad as one patch to begin with. Merging quads into larger patches along the lines that join irregular vertices cut one test from 5,600 patches to 78 in 0.16 s ([quad layout extraction, CAD&A 2016](https://www.cad-journal.net/files/vol_13/CAD_13%283%29_2016_409-416.pdf)), but that is a later refinement. No runtime figures for the Python binding on meshes of 100,000 triangles were found, so time it early.

### Flatten with a harmonic map, share the boundary curves, skip the trim

Projecting a patch onto a plane folds as soon as the patch curls past a quarter turn. A harmonic map does not: pin the four corners of the patch to the corners of the unit square, space the border vertices along the sides by length, and solve for the interior so that every vertex sits at the average of its neighbours. With a convex border no triangle can flip, and the resulting coordinates are directly the (u, v) domain of the spline. libigl provides the pieces as `boundary_loop` and `harmonic` ([libigl tutorial](https://libigl.github.io/libigl-python-bindings/tut-chapter4/)). Because the domain is the full square, the face needs no trimming wire: `BRepBuilderAPI_MakeFace(surface, tolerance)` uses the surface's natural bounds, and the failures tied to pcurves, wire orientation and self-intersection cannot arise.

Borders come first and are shared. For each layout edge, resample the mesh polyline and fit one curve with `GeomAPI_PointsToBSpline`, which guarantees every point lies within the requested 3D tolerance and has a variant that also penalises length, curvature and torsion, which removes the zig-zag ([GeomAPI_PointsToBSpline](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_geom_a_p_i___points_to_b_spline.html)). Use 0.05 mm on feature edges and 0.3 mm elsewhere. Both neighbouring patches take that curve as their boundary row of control points, which is how Mosbach's method achieves watertightness, and the interior is then an ordinary sparse least-squares solve with a light smoothing term. Start at 6×6 to 8×8 control points and insert knots only where error exceeds tolerance; Eck and Hoppe used 11×11. No source states how many control points an organic figure needs at 0.3 mm against 0.05 mm, so the error-driven loop has to discover it. Várady's mechanical-part cases needed about 120,000 control points for 800,000 points at better than 0.1 mm ([Várady](https://www.academia.edu/11977222/Fast_and_accurate_NURBS_fitting_for_reverse_engineering)), which argues for keeping the tight tolerance at feature edges and the loose one inside.

When neighbouring faces share the very same edge object, sewing has nothing to do. mesh2step measured shared-topology construction at **2.6–3.2 times faster** than build-then-sew for its faces ([mesh2step](https://github.com/tommasobbianchi/mesh2step)), and OCCT's `BRepBuilderAPI_FastSewing` exists for patches with "the same parameterization along common boundaries" precisely because it can skip the slow edge-consistency check ([FastSewing reference](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_builder_a_p_i___fast_sewing.html)). Smoothness across borders can stay approximate. Exact tangent continuity (G1, meaning no crease) is not generally achievable with ordinary bicubic patches on an arbitrary quad layout, and Mosbach's penalty approach still reaches average tangent errors of 0.005–0.016° ([Mosbach et al. 2020](https://web.cs.ucdavis.edu/~hamann/MosbachSchladitzHamannHagenComputingAndInformationScienceInEngineeringPaperAsAcceptedFinal08072020.pdf)). STEP importers require a closed shell with edges inside tolerance, not G1.

### A trimming recipe and a cheap fallback ladder for what remains

Some regions stay trimmed: analytic faces with irregular borders, and odd patches the layout cannot make four-sided. The roughly 150 current failures most likely split three ways. Zig-zag borders give self-intersecting wires. Edges traced along the mesh do not lie on a surface that is deliberately up to 0.3 mm away, which breaks the "same parameter" agreement between an edge's 3D and 2D curves. Folded parametrisations give pcurves that double back. Log the `BRepCheck` status of every failed face to confirm that split before fixing anything. The recipe OCCT users converge on is to derive the trimming curve in the surface's own (u, v) space, build the 3D edge from it, keep it inside the surface's parameter range, call `MakeFace(surface, wire, False)`, retry with the wire reversed if the face comes out inverted, and then run `ShapeFix_Face`; one forum case was solved by clamping to the parameter range and another simply by reversing the wire ([OCCT forum, trimmed NURBS](https://occt3d.com/dev/content/trimmed-nurbs-surface-creation/index.html); [OCCT forum, wire direction](https://occt3d.com/dev/content/trimming-b-spline-surface-bspline-curve-0/index.html)). Set the fixing precision to the fit error, since OCCT's healing tools repair the same-parameter flag when "curve deviation is greater than the edge tolerance" ([Shape Healing guide](https://dev.opencascade.org/doc/overview/html/occt_user_guides__shape_healing.html)).

The fallback ladder then changes character. A failing four-sided patch gets a Coons fill, a surface interpolated in closed form from its four border curves, via `GeomFill_BSplineCurves` ([GeomFill_BSplineCurves](https://dev.opencascade.org/doc/refman/html/class_geom_fill___b_spline_curves.html)). It ignores interior data, so re-check its deviation, but it costs almost nothing. The plate solver is reserved for the few holes with five or more sides, with reduced settings; around 8 points per edge, one iteration, maximum degree 5 and 4 segments are starting values to tune, since no published timings against these parameters exist. Triangles become the exception. Commercial auto-surfacing lands in the same place: QuickSurface's version is described as "a brute force approach" producing "hundreds, if not thousands" of patches and struggling around sharp edges ([DEVELOP3D review](https://www.mesh2surface.com/media/D3D_OCT19_QuickSurface.pdf)), which is the reason to claim prismatic surface exactly before this stage runs.

## Commercial tools confirm the architecture; ML supplies ideas, not code

Every commercial tool splits the problem the same way. Fusion's Convert Mesh has a Faceted mode with one face per triangle, a Prismatic mode that "merges groups of faces in prismatic features into singular faces" and an Organic mode that fits a T-spline body with a user-set face budget ([Autodesk help](https://help.autodesk.com/view/fusion360/ENU/?guid=MESH-CONVERT-TO-SOLID)); the Organic default is **100 faces** ([Product Design Online](https://productdesignonline.com/how-to-convert-a-3d-scan-to-t-splines-in-fusion-360-kevin-kennedy/)), and Prismatic mode froze on an organic leaf that Faceted mode converted ([Autodesk forum](https://forums.autodesk.com/t5/fusion-design-validate-document/fusion-freezes-when-converting-mesh-with-prismatic/td-p/10559436)). Design X's Auto Segment divides a scan into planes, cylinders and cones, and its Auto Surface wraps a model in four-sided NURBS patches ([Hawk Ridge](https://hawkridgesys.com/blog/hybrid-modeling-with-geomagic-design-x)). SpaceClaim's Auto Skin describes exactly the hybrid proposed here: fitted CAD faces "will be trimmed to the facet regions", and triangles matching no CAD face "will be autoskinned" ([SpaceClaim help](https://help.spaceclaim.com/dsm/6.0/en/Discovery/user_manual/tools/t_rev-eng_auto-skin_create-cad-geom.html)). No vendor publishes face counts, tolerances or run times, so a project that reports measured deviation, face count and time on named hardware is offering something the paid tools do not.

Machine-learning methods divide into segmentation networks, direct B-rep predictors and CAD-program generators, and none fits a laptop tool. The segmenters work on fixed samples of 8,000–10,000 points and have no torus class ([HPNet, ICCV 2021](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2021/papers/Yan_HPNet_Deep_Primitive_Segmentation_Using_Hybrid_Representations_ICCV_2021_paper.pdf)), so small holes and fillets disappear before the network sees them. Point2CAD, the most complete pipeline, fits planes, spheres, cylinders and cones in closed form and uses a neural surface for the rest, calls itself "sensitive to the quality of the point cloud segmentation" ([Point2CAD, CVPR 2024](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Liu_Point2CAD_Reverse_Engineering_CAD_Models_from_3D_Point_Clouds_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf)), and is licensed CC-BY-NC for "personal and research use only" ([point2cad repository](https://github.com/prs-eth/point2cad)). Split-and-Fit is GPL-3.0 and wants CUDA with 64 GB of shared memory ([NVDNet](https://github.com/yilinliu77/NVDNet)). ComplexGen averages ten minutes per model and needs a Gurobi licence ([ComplexGen](https://github.com/guohaoxiang/ComplexGen)). NURBSFit requires an NVIDIA GPU and outputs meshes, not STEP ([NURBSFit](https://github.com/lizOnly/nurbsfit)). CAD-Recode writes sketch-and-extrude code only, lacks "revolution and fillet", and its overlap score drops **from 92.0% on synthetic data to 74.2% on real scans** ([CAD-Recode](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.14042)). Recognition is not where networks win anyway: in the SHREC contest a classical Hough-voting entry classified 96.8% of shapes correctly against 99.4% for the best network, and the weakness several entries shared was unstable fitting ([SHREC 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.07636)). All of this supports your conclusion that cores matter more than a GPU: the reusable content is the selection rule, the fit-oversized-then-intersect strategy and the evaluation metrics, and none of it needs a network.

## Batching, shared edges and processes recover the remaining minutes

Threads do not help. The maintainer of the OCP binding states that "OCP does not release the GIL", and a user who tried 24 threads found the run slower than serial ([CadQuery group](https://groups.google.com/g/cadquery/c/zaJ-hUKwKmY)); the route that works is separate processes, where one developer reported **4–8 times speed-up on 16 cores** with final assembly still on a single core ([CadQuery issue #579](https://github.com/CadQuery/cadquery/issues/579)). Start workers with the "spawn" method: after OCCT has run a parallel mesh, a forked child inherits a thread pool without its threads and the next parallel call "blocks forever" ([adacpp PR #58](https://github.com/Krande/adacpp/pull/58)). The cleanest division gives workers only NumPy work, the harmonic solve and least-squares fit for one patch, returning control-point arrays, while the parent process builds the OCCT faces. Workers then never load OCCT, which keeps memory low on an 8 GB machine; cap the pool at about four workers there and measure peak memory, since no per-process figures were found. Inside one process, OCCT's own parallelism is opt-in: a flag on `BRepMesh_IncrementalMesh`, a flag on `BRepCheck_Analyzer`, and `SetRunParallel` on booleans, the last of which one downstream project disabled "after causing silent data corruption" and should be verified on your data before use ([OCCTSwift issue #342](https://github.com/SecondMouseAU/OCCTSwift/issues/342)).

Checks should be batched. `BRepCheck_Analyzer` has a topology-only mode, a sampled distance check that is the faster default, and a parallel switch ([BRepCheck_Analyzer reference](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_check___analyzer.html)); run it once on the finished shell, and send only the flagged faces to `ShapeFix`. Stop tessellating faces to measure deviation. The profile quoted in the research brief shows about 92 s in 2,600 per-face `BRepMesh` calls and 95 s in 2,600 `closest_point` calls, and both vanish if each face's surface is evaluated on a small (u, v) grid, all samples are pooled into one array, and one distance query is made. The library matters enormously: `trimesh.proximity.closest_point` took **32 s for 500,000 queries where pysdf took 0.059 s** ([trimesh issue #1116](https://github.com/mikedh/trimesh/issues/1116)), and pysdf sustains 1.67 million queries per second against a 179,000-vertex mesh on a six-core 8th-generation i7 ([pysdf](https://github.com/sxyu/sdf)). pysdf is approximate, checking only faces next to the nearest vertex, so use libigl's exact `point_mesh_squared_distance` or point-cloud-utils when you need the closest triangle index for region mapping ([point-cloud-utils](https://fwilliams.info/point-cloud-utils/sections/closest_point_on_mesh/)).

Assembly and output hold the last savings. `ShapeUpgrade_UnifySameDomain` took **75–90 s on a 465-face file**, and could loop forever on periodic surfaces in OCCT 7.8.1, fixed in 8.0 ([pythonocc issue #1477](https://github.com/tpaviot/pythonocc-core/issues/1477)). It merges faces only when they lie on identical underlying surfaces ([UnifySameDomain reference](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_shape_upgrade___unify_same_domain.html)), so it can do nothing for a patch network, and it has turned valid shapes invalid ([OCCT issue #1541](https://github.com/Open-Cascade-SAS/OCCT/issues/1541)). Run it only on the analytic subset, after merging by snapped parameters yourself, and re-validate. Moving to the `cadquery-ocp` 8.0.1 wheels, 48–68 MB per platform for Python 3.11–3.14, picks up that fix ([cadquery-ocp on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/cadquery-ocp/)). For file size, low-degree patches with few knots matter most, and OCCT's `write.surfacecurve.mode = 0` writes STEP without pcurves, which "decreases the size of the resulting STEP file" at the price of work for the importer; offer it as a compact option, not the default ([OCCT STEP guide](https://github.com/Open-Cascade-SAS/OCCT/blob/master/dox/user_guides/step/step.md)). Avoid per-face colours on large files, since one hour-long FreeCAD import was traced partly to a colour lookup whose total cost grows with the square of the entity count ([FreeCAD issue #18735](https://github.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/issues/18735)). Cancellation belongs at process level. OCCT's progress interface is cooperative and FreeCAD found some code paths do not poll it often enough ([FreeCAD PR #19763](https://github.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/pull/19763)), so a Cancel button should set a flag checked between faces and terminate the worker process after a grace period.

## A staged plan: stop the waste, claim exact surface, then rebuild freeform

The order below ranks steps by expected benefit per unit of effort for this project. Effort figures are estimates for one hobbyist developer, and the pass marks are targets to test against, not forecasts. Step 1 comes first because nothing after it can be judged without it.

Steps 2–5 change no geometry and should bring the standard mode's run time down sharply on their own, since they target the plate solver and the per-face round trips directly. Steps 6–8 are where accuracy and face count move, and they reuse the two things the project already does well: the line-geometry triage and the sketch fitter. Step 9 is deliberately placed before step 10, because harmonic parametrisation and shared curves cure folding and zig-zag within the current layout and tell you how much of the failure rate was layout and how much was construction, before you commit weeks to a new layout.

Four items stay off the plan. Neural segmentation and neural surfaces bring GPU needs and licence trouble for no gain on exported meshes. General freeform sweep detection costs minutes and hand-tuned thresholds. Exact G1 across the network requires solving all patches together. A slimmer custom OCCT build would save tens of megabytes at the price of maintaining five platform builds. The main unknowns to measure rather than assume are the run time of the quad remesher on your meshes, the control-point counts that organic regions need at 0.3 mm, the robustness of trimming revolved faces against freeform neighbours, and the licences of libigl, pysdf, point-cloud-utils and the decimation packages, which this research did not verify.

## Conclusion

The project already contains its own answer in two separate modes. Extrude outline with Smooth sketch rebuilds exact geometry from a 2D profile, and the line-geometry triage finds axes in half a second; joined together and applied per region instead of per part, they form the roughing stage. The useful mental model is a claim ladder in which each rung (plane, cylinder, revolve, extrude, fillet, four-sided patch, Coons fill, plate, triangle) must prove itself against the full-resolution mesh or pass its region down, and each replacement is verified locally the moment it is made. Under that model the split between standard and Guesstimate modes dissolves: tolerance becomes a property of the region, 0.05 mm where a surface is exact and 0.3 mm where it is organic, and the mode switch stops being an opt-in compromise.

Two implications follow. Because vendors publish no numbers at all, the benchmark harness is more than internal tooling: a table of measured deviation, face count and time per test model on a named laptop is the project's most credible public claim. And the risk has moved. Fitting is no longer the hard part; the junction where an exact face meets a patch is, because it is the one place the research found recipes but no measurements. Building a small test file that isolates that junction, before the freeform rebuild, is the cheapest insurance in the plan.
