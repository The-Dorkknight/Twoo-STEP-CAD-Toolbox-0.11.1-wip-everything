## Count normals and slices, not pixels

The cheap machine vision that pays off for STL2STEP is not image processing. It is the vision toolbox (histograms, voting, contour tracing, connected regions) applied to the mesh's own exact data: its facet normals and its vector cross-sections. A histogram of facet normals exposes every plane, extrusion direction and cylinder axis; building it took tens of milliseconds for 200,000 facets in a research sandbox, the axis voting that follows took under a second, and the result hands the experimental Staged engine the several-features-at-once hypotheses that a single whole-part test cannot produce. Cutting the part at the heights of its flat faces and fitting lines and arcs to each section rebuilds prismatic parts as exact extrusions; one open-source converter under your own licence family already works this way. Rendered depth, normal and face-ID images earn a place for three narrow jobs only: naming pockets, bosses and through-holes along an axis, coping with messy multi-shell meshes, and checking the finished STEP against the STL. They cannot dimension anything: a 1024-pixel render locates a 3 mm hole to roughly 5–10 µm after correction, a vector section to 1–3 µm, and the mesh vertices exactly, while a single such render costs several times more than building the normal histogram. Learned models are not worth doing on a GPU-less i3: the small ones are slow, brittle or unlicensed, and the capable ones need gigabytes, minutes per part and a GPU, and still drop small features. Two cautions apply throughout. Every millisecond figure for the methods proposed here comes from a 2-core cloud Xeon and one synthetic test plate, not from a 7th-generation i3; and several of the best ideas are reasoned adaptations that no paper has benchmarked on CAD-exported meshes.

## The mesh already holds what a camera would have to guess

"Machine vision" covers four different things in this report, and they deserve different verdicts. The first is **parameter-space voting**: every facet casts a vote for the shapes it could belong to, and peaks in the vote count reveal the shapes. The Hough transform is the classic example, and the Gauss map (a plot of every facet's normal direction as a point on a unit sphere) is the cheapest version. The second is **section and silhouette analysis**: cut or project the part into 2D outlines and reason about those. The third is **true image space**: render the mesh into pixel images (depth, normal direction, or the ID of the facet behind each pixel) and run 2D detectors such as edge finders, circle finders and connected-region labelling. The fourth is **learned models** that were trained on thousands of examples.

The dividing line between them is information. Rendering throws information away: the standard course notes on line drawing from 3D models state that data discarded during rendering cannot be reconstructed from the 2D image alone ([Hertzmann 1999](https://mrl.cs.nyu.edu/publications/npr-course1999/hertzmann-intro3d.pdf)), and the paper that introduced per-pixel geometry buffers notes that a buffer holds only one surface per pixel and that real discontinuities are hard to tell from steep continuous change ([Saito and Takahashi 1990](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall00/cs597b/papers/saito90.pdf)). A clean CAD-exported STL, by contrast, already contains exact facet normals, exact adjacency between triangles, and vertices that the exporter placed on the true design surface ([OCCT mesh guide](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/overview/html/occt_user_guides__mesh.html)). Vision techniques were invented for cameras and laser scanners that have none of that. The practical rule that falls out of all four research threads is **image to find and classify, mesh to measure**: use vision-style counting to propose what is there, then compute every number that reaches the STEP file from the original vertices.

## A normal histogram finds planes, axes and extrusion directions in milliseconds

### Every surface type leaves a fingerprint on the normal sphere

Take each facet's normal, treat it as a point on a unit sphere, and weight it by the facet's area. Horn's 1984 paper defined this "extended Gaussian image" and showed it ignores where the object sits in space ([Horn 1984](https://people.csail.mit.edu/bkph/papers/EGI.pdf)). The fingerprints are simple. **A plane is a single point. A cylinder is a great circle** (an equator-like ring) whose pole is the cylinder's axis. A cone is a smaller ring at a fixed latitude. Published segmenters for mechanical CAD meshes use exactly this: Le and Duan find "poles" (planes) and "rings" (cylinders and cones) on the sphere and use them as the part's major directions before doing anything else ([Le and Duan 2017](https://duanye.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/CAGD-2017-compressed.pdf)), and PGP2X reads a cylinder's axis from the plane its normals lie in, then fits a 2D circle for position and radius, on a test set of 620 CAD models ([PGP2X 2015](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2015/53564/53564.pdf)). Spheres and tori smear into 2D patches, and freeform surface into irregular blobs.

Two consequences matter for STL2STEP. First, the ring test is broader than "cylinder": any extrusion, round or not, puts all its wall normals on one great circle, and any constant-draft pocket puts them on one small circle. The histogram therefore cannot tell a round hole from a hexagonal one, but it is precisely the extrusion-direction detector the Staged engine wants, for prisms and cylinders alike. That reading is an inference from the cited geometry, not a quoted result. Second, the sphere throws away position, so every published method follows it with a small positional step: a one-number offset histogram for planes, 2D circle detection for cylinders, or connected regions on the mesh.

How the histogram is stored matters. A latitude-longitude grid has tiny cells near the poles, which biases voting toward the equator and, in one review's test, failed completely on a cube aligned with the coordinate axes ([Borrmann et al. 2011](https://robotik.informatik.uni-wuerzburg.de/telematics/download/3dresearch2011.pdf)). Axis-aligned parts are the normal case for CAD, so that failure would hit most inputs. For clean CAD meshes there is a better option than any grid, though it is an inference and not a published method: flat faces produce exactly repeated normals, so rounding normals to four decimal places and merging duplicates with summed area gives an exact, short list of directions with no grid at all. In the sandbox, merging 200,000 normals this way took about 32 ms and a 3×3 summary matrix with its eigen-solve took about 8 ms.

### Planes need a direction peak and a one-number offset, not RANSAC

RANSAC, the converter's current workhorse, guesses a shape from a few random points and counts agreement. Hough-style voting replaces the guessing with counting, and published timings show what that buys when votes are cast once per flat cluster instead of once per point. On a 2011 desktop i7, the kernel-based 3D Hough transform found the planes in a 68,000-point scan in **0.022 s against 0.340 s for Efficient RANSAC**, and in a 20-million-point scan in 2.1 s against 745 s ([Limberger and Oliveira 2015](https://www.inf.ufrgs.br/~oliveira/pubs_files/HT3D/Limberger_Oliveira_3D_HT_Pre-Print_low_res.pdf)). An earlier review measured randomized Hough at 0.67 s against 7.6 s for region growing on a 325,000-point room scan, but on a cluttered 145,000-point scene the order reversed, 14.0 s against 2.7 s ([Borrmann et al. 2011](https://robotik.informatik.uni-wuerzburg.de/telematics/download/3dresearch2011.pdf)). A lidar study that let every point vote into a full 3D grid found Hough slower and fussier than RANSAC and chose RANSAC ([Tarsha-Kurdi et al. 2007](https://www.isprs.org/proceedings/XXXVI/3-W52/final_papers/Tarsha-Kurdi_2007.pdf)). The verdicts conflict for a reason: voting wins when a few dominant structures explain the data and votes are pre-grouped, which describes a CAD part and does not describe a cluttered room.

For exact facet normals the plane detector collapses into three array operations: find the heavy directions in the merged-normal list; for the facets of each direction, histogram the single number that says how far along that direction they sit; split what remains into connected pieces over triangle adjacency. Each facet votes once, there is no randomness, and the result is the same on every run. The published timings are for laser scans on other hardware, and the three-step recipe is this research's adaptation, so treat "strictly cheaper than RANSAC plane extraction" as a well-founded expectation to confirm in your own benchmark rather than as a published fact.

### Cylinders and holes: clear the planes, vote for the axis, then find circles

A full cylinder search has five unknowns, and a brute-force voting grid for it would need about 7×10¹² cells. Rabbani and van den Heuvel made it practical by splitting it: first vote for the axis direction on the normal sphere, then project the points onto the plane across that axis and vote for a circle's centre and radius ([Rabbani and van den Heuvel 2005](https://www.isprs.org/proceedings/xxxvi/3-w19/papers/060.pdf)). The same paper documents the method's main trap: large flat areas produce clusters that interfere with axis estimation, so the authors removed planes first. A later paper softens that by weighting votes by curvature, which kept axis error stable with twice as much flat clutter as cylinder ([Figueiredo et al. 2017](https://vislab.isr.tecnico.ulisboa.pt/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/rfigueiredo-icarsc2017.pdf)).

The sandbox reproduced both the trap and the cure on a synthetic mix of a box and one 48-segment cylinder. Without plane removal, the strongest "axis" was a box edge direction. After dropping the six plane peaks, voting 20,000 distinct normals against 2,562 candidate directions took about 0.64 s and landed within 1.5° of the true axis; a 3×3 eigen-solve on the winners then took 0.4 ms and cut the error below 0.01°. Circle voting for 60,000 projected points over 117 candidate radii took about 0.35 s and recovered the radius exactly. A real CAD part has hundreds to a few thousand distinct normals, so the first step should be proportionally cheaper. The pattern to copy is **coarse voting to choose the facets, a closed-form fit to set the numbers**. That removes the standard objection to Hough methods, that their accuracy is limited by the grid's cell size ([Limberger and Oliveira 2015](https://www.inf.ufrgs.br/~oliveira/pubs_files/HT3D/Limberger_Oliveira_3D_HT_Pre-Print_low_res.pdf)).

This is also how voting and the Staged engine should divide the work. The engine's 6×6 line-geometry test and the related "slippage" analysis classify one region by one small matrix calculation, declaring an eigenvalue small when it sits a factor of 100–300 below the largest ([Gelfand and Guibas 2004](https://geometry.stanford.edu/lgl_2024/papers/gg-ssulsa-04/gg-ssulsa-04.pdf)). That test returns one dominant motion. A part with two turned features on different, non-parallel axes has no single motion that fits, so the whole-part test sees nothing. Voting on the normal sphere is naturally multi-feature: it proposes each axis direction with its own set of supporting facets, and the 6×6 solve on each set confirms the type, adds the axis position and refines the direction. A useful cue follows, though it is untested: a turned feature's normals form rings and bands all centred on one pole, so a normal histogram that looks rotationally symmetric about a direction flags a lathe axis before any positional work is done. Once a direction is known, every hole along it is a 2D circle problem and every flat across it is a one-number problem, which is the saving Le and Duan exploit by slicing only across the major directions ([Le and Duan 2017](https://duanye.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/CAGD-2017-compressed.pdf)). Their runtimes of 88–595 s were for 850,000 to 1.9 million sample points with circle detection in every slice, and their uniform slice spacing can miss features, so borrow the idea and not the implementation.

### Cones, tori and fillets stay per-region problems

The neat two-step split does not extend to cones, whose apex adds an unknown, or to tori. The published route is to isolate a region first, move it to a standard pose, and then vote over the one or two numbers that remain: one for a sphere, cylinder or cone, two for a torus ([Raffo et al. 2022](https://iris.cnr.it/retrieve/f79a413b-49ef-4b79-804a-333a1976c94d/2205.15426v2.pdf)). That suits STL2STEP, because sharp-edge region growing already produces regions. Accuracy is good but not perfect: in the SHREC 2022 contest this training-free Hough method classified **96.76% of primitives correctly against 99.35% for the best neural network** ([SHREC 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.07636)), and in the method's own paper tori had the lowest true-positive rate, 0.58–0.60 ([Raffo et al. 2022](https://iris.cnr.it/retrieve/f79a413b-49ef-4b79-804a-333a1976c94d/2205.15426v2.pdf)). Its 123 s per shape is an artefact of dense voting grids in MATLAB and says little about a sparse NumPy version; the same group later extended the approach to surfaces of revolution and helical surfaces, and added a clustering step that merges parallel planes, coaxial cylinders and equal radii ([Romanengo et al. 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2301.04371)).

Fillets are the weak spot of every cheap technique in this report. On the normal sphere a constant-radius fillet between two flat faces is the arc joining their two points, which is a usable cue, but it is an inference. Sharp-edge segmentation cannot separate a fillet from the faces it blends into, because the angle across the true boundary is no larger than the angle between the fillet's own facets; the 2026 paper on this states that smooth transitions are not separated ([Mathematics 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7390/14/5/757)). Images are worse: a 90° fillet arc in a silhouette is far short of the roughly 165° of arc that a leading ellipse detector needs ([Lu et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.03243)). No learned model targeting fillet boundaries on meshes turned up either. The most promising cheap signal is a derived one: on an exported mesh each smooth edge between two facet strips gives a radius estimate from the strip width and the turning angle, and a region where that radius is constant is a cylinder or fillet. Nobody has published or tested it, so it belongs in an experiment, not in the plan.

### Skip curvature maps; use the dihedral-angle histogram

Curvature-based labels (the "shape index" that calls a point cup-like, ridge-like or saddle-like) look like the natural per-facet image to compute, and the literature advises against it on CAD meshes. The shape index is undefined on flat surfaces by its inventors' own account ([Koenderink and van Doorn 1992](https://forums.fast.ai/uploads/default/original/2X/a/a4ef2b9b5d71c83ff887680800b0b58feae42d35.pdf)). The CAD-specific curvature segmenter removes sharp edges first because curvature is not defined on them ([Lavoué et al. 2005](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010448504002003)); another restricts curvature to the densely triangulated blend regions and handles the long thin triangles of planes and cylinders on the normal sphere instead ([Xiao et al. 2011](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0097849311000598)); and the 2026 paper says outright that discrete curvature is highly sensitive to mesh connectivity on CAD models, where curvature vanishes almost everywhere and is singular at creases ([Mathematics 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7390/14/5/757)). That paper's replacement is the one STL2STEP already uses: threshold the dihedral angle (the angle between neighbouring triangles) and label connected regions, in time proportional to face count, **0.43 s for 13,000 faces and 2.24 s for 64,000 in pure Python**.

One refinement is worth adding, as an inference to verify. A histogram of all dihedral angles in an exported mesh should show three groups: zero (coplanar, merge), a spike at the exporter's angular step (the facets of smooth curved faces), and large angles (real sharp edges). The spike's position reveals the exporter's setting and gives a principled per-file threshold in place of a fixed 30°.

### Snapping and symmetry work on a few hundred primitives, not on triangles

Making near-parallel faces exactly parallel and near-equal radii exactly equal is what turns good fits into clean CAD. The famous method, GlobFit, enforces orientation, then placement, then equality, by constrained optimisation, and took **227–876 s for 21–56 primitives** because it fights scanner noise ([GlobFit 2011](https://graphics.stanford.edu/~niloy/research/globFit/paper_docs/globFit_sigg11_small.pdf)). The cheap alternative in the CAD-beautification literature is to cluster the fitted primitives' own parameters and replace each cluster with its area-weighted mean ([Gauthier et al. 2019](https://www.lirmm.fr/~subsol/WWW/GRAPP.0219.1.pdf); [Langbein 2003](https://langbein.org/wp-content/uploads/2008/10/beautification.pdf)). Following GlobFit's order with that cheaper machinery gives a solver-free sequence: cluster directions and average them; snap near-square clusters square; refit each primitive with its direction fixed, which turns a plane into an average of offsets and a cylinder into a 2D circle fit; then cluster axis positions (coaxial), plane offsets (coplanar) and radii (equal). Hole patterns fall out of the same data, since holes sharing a direction are 2D points whose spacing or common circle is trivial to test for tens of holes. The sequence is assembled by inference, and the notes found **no published tolerances for snapping on noise-free CAD meshes**. GlobFit's 15° default would be destructive here; thresholds must sit well below real draft angles of 1–3°, or the snap will straighten a deliberate taper.

Symmetry detection by voting is a case where the famous methods are the wrong ones. Mitra, Guibas and Pauly's method discards every sample without two distinct curvatures, which means flat and cylindrical areas, most of a machined part, and it took 74–289 s on meshes of 160,000–173,000 vertices ([Mitra et al. 2006](https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~jacobson/seminar/mitra-et-al-2006.pdf)). The planar reflective symmetry transform took about 8 s at a coarse 64³ resolution ([Podolak et al. 2006](https://gfx.cs.princeton.edu/pubs/Podolak_2006_APS/symmpaper.pdf)). Work on real CAD models instead compares whole faces by type, area and position, and reports an F1 score of 0.94 in time proportional to the number of faces ([Symmetry 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-8994/15/5/1058)). The mesh equivalent, an inference, is to let pairs of matching fitted primitives vote for the mirror plane between them, then confirm by reflecting a few thousand vertices and checking they land on the mesh. Its value is as a constraint and a labour saver (fit one half, mirror the result), so it ranks below detection and snapping.

On licences, the capable existing libraries are closed to you. CGAL's shape detection and plane regularisation are GPL ([CGAL reference](https://doc.cgal.org/5.0.3/Shape_detection/group__PkgShapeDetectionRef.html)) and the kernel-Hough depth-image code is GPL-3.0 ([D-KHT](https://github.com/eduardovera/D-KHT)). The permissive options are narrow: FastGaussianAccumulator is MIT but finds plane normals only ([FastGA](https://github.com/JeremyBYU/FastGaussianAccumulator)), and pyRANSAC-3D is Apache-2.0 but is RANSAC again ([pyRANSAC-3D](https://github.com/leomariga/pyRANSAC-3D)). Papers' algorithms are free to reimplement, and everything in this section reduces to histograms, small eigen-solves and SciPy clustering, so writing it in-house in NumPy is both the legal route and the simplest one.

## Sections and silhouettes rebuild prismatic parts with a handful of booleans

### Three silhouettes give a safe first solid with one-sided error

Reconstructing a solid from the three views of an engineering drawing is an old research field with two families. The wireframe family is general but spends its effort on a search to eliminate "ghost" edges, which one recent system describes as growing as 2 to the power of the number of assumed faces ([IntechOpen 2020](https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/72385)). The volume family is the one that transfers: sweep each view's outer outline along its viewing direction, intersect the three prisms into a "basic solid", then subtract volumes built from the interior loops ([Shum et al. 2001](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010448500000798)). Its stated limits are faces inclined to all three view planes and features with no matching loops in the other views ([Dimri and Gurumoorthy 2005](https://www.academia.edu/34678576/Handling_sectional_views_in_volume_based_approach_to_automatically_construct_3D_solid_from_2D_views)); turned features are handled by a hint, circles in one view plus a symmetric profile in the others, and only when the axis lies along a view direction ([Lee and Han 2005](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010448505000205)).

No source applies these recipes to views of a mesh, so the mapping is an inference, but it is a short one: run Extrude outline along X, Y and Z, build three prisms, and take their common volume with two boolean operations. Every wall comes from a fitted line or arc, so the faces are true planes and cylinders. The ghost problem that dominates the drawing literature does not arise, because any candidate solid can be tested against the mesh itself. The result is exact for plates with through-holes and outline cut-outs, brackets, and blocks with through-slots. For everything else it is too big and never too small, because silhouette intersection cannot recover a concavity hidden from all views, however many views are added ([Slabaugh et al. 2001](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~allen/PHOTOPAPERS/SlabaughSurveyVolum.pdf)). That one-sided error is useful: the volume difference between this solid and the mesh is a complete, cheap test of whether more work is needed.

### Slab decomposition is the deterministic form of Loft mode

Blind pockets, counterbores and steps live inside the outline, and the cheapest robust way to capture them is to cut the part into slabs. Flat faces square to an axis mark the heights where the cross-section can change; between two neighbouring heights a prismatic part's section is constant; so one section per slab, fitted with lines and arcs and extruded over the slab's height, reproduces the part. The open-source converter stlToSolid implements exactly this for STL input: it clusters face normals on the Gauss sphere to propose axes, lets planar faces vote for height levels, splits each section outline into lines and circular arcs, snaps radii and centres by clustering them across all slabs, extrudes constant slabs and lofts varying ones, and falls back to per-face fitting when a deviation gate fails. Its README reports a servo bracket going **from 1,644 triangles to 23 faces at 0.060 mm maximum deviation**, and a 68-body file taking about 18 minutes with four workers ([stlToSolid](https://github.com/Crypto69/stlToSolid)). Those are the author's own untested figures. The project is licensed PolyForm Noncommercial 1.0.0, the same family as yours, which makes it readable as a reference; copying code would bind the result to that author's terms, so check the licence text before lifting anything.

For STL2STEP this is less a new mode than a tightening of two existing ones. Loft mode today places sections where the shape seems to change; slab decomposition places them at exact flat-face heights, extrudes wherever the two ends of a slab agree, and keeps lofting only for slabs that genuinely vary. The global clustering step is not optional: without it, the same hole fitted in two slabs yields two slightly different cylinders and a sliver face between them. Classical reverse-engineering research describes the same ladder (sections fitted with lines, arcs and splines, then sweeping or skinning) as its "sectional feature" strategy ([Ke et al. 2006](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010448506000169)), and commercial tools stop well short of automating it: Fusion makes the user pick a curve type and click endpoints segment by segment ([Fusion help](https://help.autodesk.com/cloudhelp/ENU/Fusion-Sketch/files/SKT-FIT-CURVE-TO-MESH-SECTION.htm)).

The limits are clear. Slicing sees only features aligned with the slicing axis; a cross-drilled hole shows up as a notch in a few slices, and stlToSolid handles cross-axis bores separately as fitted cylinders and cones. Running the decomposition along each main direction and intersecting the results extends coverage, and the leftover volume points at what remains. How many real parts this covers is not known: no benchmark measures the share of real CAD parts that are exactly stacked extrusions. The nearest figure is that 18 fixed extrusion combinations cover about 80% of the DeepCAD dataset in a learned prismatic decomposer, which itself reaches only 79% volume overlap and is therefore the opposite trade-off to a tolerance-driven converter ([Lambourne et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.01161)).

### Inner loops of a section are the pockets and holes

Two mesh-based feature recognition papers supply detection rules that fit the slicing code. The random cutting plane approach rests on one observation: a plane through a part gives an outer contour, and the triangles of any hole, slot or pocket crossing the plane produce inner contours. It needs no angle or curvature thresholds and was tested on 40 engineering parts, though only its abstract was readable and no code was found ([Muraleedharan et al. 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0097849317301188)). With planes placed at slab mid-heights instead of at random, the inner loops are also the sketch profiles of the pockets to cut, so recognition and reconstruction share one computation; that pairing is an inference. The slice-based approach of Adhikary and Gurumoorthy covers features that open onto the outline and therefore make no inner loop: a run of inward corners bounded by outward corners marks a depression. It ran in **about 10 s for 80,000 triangles** and cannot handle intersecting features ([Adhikary and Gurumoorthy 2016](https://www.cad-journal.net/files/vol_13/CAD_13(5)_2016_587-599.pdf)). Both methods only label triangles; neither outputs CAD geometry.

Classical machining-feature recognition proper, which matches patterns in a graph of faces and their concave or convex joins, needs a finished B-rep. That makes it a quality check after conversion, not a conversion step. Analysis Situs does this on OpenCASCADE under a 3-clause BSD licence ([Analysis Situs FAQ](https://analysissitus.org/faq.html)), and its authors report recognition in fractions of a second on a 4,906-face model ([Slyadnev et al. 2020](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2744/paper70.pdf)). Run on your output, it can confirm that each hole is one cylindrical face and flag pockets that came out as slivers.

### Vector sections beat pixel slices on cost and accuracy

Every source that rebuilds parts from sections works on vector outlines, and the sandbox measurements show why. A single vector section of the 3,400-triangle test plate took 6 ms and 150 sections took about 1.6 s. A least-squares circle fit on a section recovered hole radii within 0.9–3.2 µm, the remaining error being the facet chords themselves; fitting on the original vertices removes even that. A raster slice needed a 1 s all-hits ray cast first and is limited to pixel accuracy. The MIT-licensed trimesh library already provides sectioning and a path simplifier that merges collinear segments and converts closed loops into circles ([trimesh path docs](https://trimesh.org/trimesh.path.simplify.html)). Rasterised slices are worth having only when a mesh is too broken to section cleanly.

Several heavier section-based and program-inference methods are not worth pursuing. Clustering "prominent cross-sections" into sweeps took 8 s on a 1,500-vertex part and 924 s on a 55,000-vertex part, with 5–15% spurious sections ([Goyal et al. 2012](https://engineering.purdue.edu/cdesign/wp/wp-content/uploads/2012/05/reverse_3d_modeling_cad2012.pdf)). Zone graphs need the B-rep you are trying to produce and take a median 242 s per model ([Xu et al. 2021](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2021/papers/Xu_Inferring_CAD_Modeling_Sequences_Using_Zone_Graphs_CVPR_2021_paper.pdf)). InverseCSG needs a Java program-synthesis toolchain ([InverseCSG](https://github.com/yijiangh/InverseCSG)).

## Rendered images classify and verify, but cannot dimension

### A height map names pockets, bosses and through-holes in milliseconds

A height map is a top-down image in which each pixel stores how far a ray travels before it hits the part. On the sandbox test plate (100 × 60 × 10 mm with six through-holes, a pocket and a boss), a histogram of pixel heights returned exactly the three flat levels in 2.6 ms at 1024×1024, and labelling connected regions by level took 15 ms. Through-holes appear as background regions, pockets as low regions enclosed by higher ones, bosses as high regions. That is a complete, naive 2.5D feature recogniser, and it has one genuine advantage over mesh methods: a depth buffer silently merges overlapping shells, so a part made of three un-unioned bodies still gave the correct map.

The caveats are equally plain. **No published work was found that does classical feature recognition from a rendered height map of a mesh**; the established uses of such maps are machining simulation and mould undercut analysis, and classical feature recognition targets B-rep models ([Lim et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.03167)). The evidence is one easy synthetic part. And on a clean mesh the flat levels are available exactly, without rendering, from the positions of faces whose normal lies along the axis. The image contributes the enclosure logic (what is a pocket and what is a step), not the dimensions. The face-ID image, which records which triangle lies behind each pixel, is what makes this usable: it maps each image region back to mesh faces so the fit happens on vertices. Ray-casting libraries return hit distance, triangle ID and normal directly on the CPU ([Open3D RaycastingScene](https://www.open3d.org/docs/release/python_api/open3d.t.geometry.RaycastingScene.html)).

### Circle detection: edge chains beat Hough, and both lose to a fit on vertices

On the same top view at 1024×1024, the sandbox compared detectors. OpenCV's standard Hough circle finder took 7.5 ms, found the seven circles with centre errors of 0.2–1.6 pixels, and added one to three false or duplicate circles at other resolutions. The "ALT" Hough variant found exactly seven but its centre error grew with circle size, to 2.7 pixels at 1024 and nearly 12 pixels at 4096. EDCircles, which chains edge pixels into arcs instead of voting, found seven of seven with **centre errors of 0.01–0.10 pixel in about 10 ms**. That agrees with its paper's claim of 10–20 ms on 640×480 images with a built-in false-detection test ([Akinlar and Topal 2013](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0031320312004268)), its reference code is MIT-licensed ([ED_Lib](https://github.com/CihanTopal/ED_Lib)), and it ships in OpenCV's contrib module ([OpenCV EdgeDrawing](https://docs.opencv.org/4.x/d1/d1c/classcv_1_1ximgproc_1_1EdgeDrawing.html)). Simple contour tracing followed by a least-squares circle fit did as well on centres (0.01–0.11 pixel in 1.1 ms) but read every radius about half a pixel small, because a traced contour runs through pixel centres; adding the half pixel back leaves roughly ±0.05 pixel.

Converted to millimetres on that plate, the image route is good to roughly 5–10 µm for a 3 mm hole at 1024 pixels, against 1–3 µm for a vector section and zero for the vertices. More pixels do not close the gap. At 4096 pixels the measured radius started to include the facet sag, 3.6 µm on that 64-segment hole, because the render is a picture of the polygon and not of the circle. Published sub-pixel edge accuracy of about 1/25 pixel assumes blurred camera edges ([Steger 1998](https://mv.in.tum.de/_media/members/steger/publications/1998/isprs-comm-iii-98-steger.pdf)), which a ray-cast render does not have. Circles also stay circles only when the view runs along the hole's axis. Any other hole becomes an ellipse, and ellipse detectors are an order of magnitude slower (0.05–0.58 s per image on a 7th-generation i7 laptop chip) and fail on thin or small ellipses ([Lu et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.03243)). Choosing the right view axes requires the normal-direction clustering from the previous section, at which point the 3D circle fit is already available. **Hough on rendered images is not worth building**; if an image detector is ever needed, use EDCircles and treat its output as a pointer to mesh faces.

Edge detection on a rendered normal map is likewise not worth building. With per-facet normals, the image edge detector fires at every facet boundary with a step equal to the dihedral angle, so it makes the same decision as the mesh threshold after an extra, lossy resampling; and a crease seen edge-on covers no pixels at all. The non-photorealistic rendering literature that invented these detectors lists the artefacts itself, including false edges from tessellation on smooth surfaces ([Saito and Takahashi 1990](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/courses/archive/fall00/cs597b/papers/saito90.pdf)). Geometry images (unwrapping the whole mesh into one square picture) were designed for compression and need a global cut and flattening that their own authors describe as leaving irregular "cruft" ([Gu, Gortler and Hoppe 2002](https://hhoppe.com/proj/gim/)); skip them too.

### Six views miss the bores; resolution cannot buy CAD tolerance

Rendering is the expensive step, not the 2D analysis. In the sandbox, ray-casting one 1024×1024 view with depth, face ID and normal took about 0.18 s, a 2048 view 0.8 s and a 4096 view 5.3 s, while almost every 2D operator at 1024 cost between 1 and 25 ms. Six axis views cost 0.6–1.7 s, and they saw only **89.9% of the test part's surface area**. The unseen tenth was exactly the interesting part: hole bore walls, pocket walls and an internal cavity. Twenty-six views reached 99.7% in about 9 s, in line with a learned multi-view segmenter's finding that about 20 viewpoints cover most man-made shapes ([Kalogerakis et al. 2017](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1612.02808)), but oblique views turn every circle into an ellipse, and the closed cavity stayed invisible. So six views can detect that a hole exists from its outline but cannot tell a straight bore from a tapered or stepped one. The classical fix is to record every surface crossing along each ray rather than the first, the idea behind layered depth-normal images ([Wang and Chen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1009.0794)); in the sandbox an all-hits cast along one axis took about 1 s at 1024 and correctly exposed the hidden cavity in a derived slice.

Resolution arithmetic settles the dimensioning question. One pixel per 0.01 mm on a 200 mm part means 20,000 pixels a side, and a depth image that size is 1.6 GB before normals. Least-squares fitting stretches a pixel roughly ten to twenty times for features that average many edge pixels, such as large circles and long straight edges, but not for a 1 mm hole about ten pixels across, a short arc or a corner. Features narrower than about two pixels (0.4 mm at 1024 pixels over 200 mm) merge or vanish, and the layered-depth paper says the same of gaps thinner than the sample spacing ([Wang and Chen](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1009.0794)). Where a slice-image method has shone, it was on a different problem: finding 353 pipes in a 232-million-point scan in 607 s against 43,252 s for RANSAC, using a trained detector, at 87.8% recall ([Lu et al. 2020](https://zhiyongsu.github.io/Pub/TVCG2021lzh.pdf)). Image space wins when the data is enormous and unstructured. For a mesh of a few thousand triangles the existing 3D pipeline is probably faster than a single render; no timing of STL2STEP's own stages was available to confirm that.

### The job images do best is checking the result

The strongest case for rendering in STL2STEP is verification, and it is a suggestion from this research with no published precedent. Tessellate the finished STEP, cast the same grid of rays through it and through the original STL along three axes, and compare where each ray enters and leaves material. A mismatch is a missed pocket, an over-cut or a lost wall, and its pixel location says where. The same comparison gives wall thickness for free and produces a "what is still unexplained" map that can seed the next feature in a staged conversion. It costs roughly a second per axis in the sandbox, grows only slowly with triangle count, and tolerates meshes that are not watertight. It complements, and does not replace, a vertex-distance check and a volume comparison, because it cannot see errors smaller than a pixel.

## Learned models cost more than they return on a GPU-less i3

The networks that label points as plane, cylinder, cone and so on look affordable by size and are not by behaviour. ParSeNet's segmentation network has only 1.25 million parameters, about 5 MB, yet a forward pass on two CPU threads took **11.8–13.4 s for 10,000 points** in the sandbox, before any clustering or fitting, because it builds a full point-to-point distance table three times; and its repository carries no licence file, so it cannot be redistributed ([ParSeNet code](https://github.com/Hippogriff/parsenet-codebase)). These networks are also brittle. When SpelsNet's authors re-tested the older ones with augmented inputs, ParSeNet's segmentation score fell from 78.2 to 34.5 and HPNet's from 40.7 to 21.7 ([SpelsNet 2024](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/028ef7e68a5ea25fc26cd6abf3a5c147-Paper-Conference.pdf)). The newest strong reconstruction method is GPL-3.0 and runs in a GPU container with 64 GB of shared memory ([NVDNet licence](https://github.com/yilinliu77/NVDNet/blob/main/LICENSE)). The one public attempt to label surface types directly on triangle meshes reached 85–92% edge accuracy, did poorly on everything except planes and cylinders, and its base method would allocate about 18 GB for a 50,000-edge mesh ([meshcnn-4-cadseg](https://atomicsulfate.github.io/meshcnn-4-cadseg/)).

Image-based learned models fare no better. Methods that lift the Segment Anything model onto meshes find semantic parts such as handles, not surface types, and are GPU workloads: about seven minutes per shape for SAMesh and 8–15 s for the fastest feed-forward alternatives, all on a GPU ([PartSAM 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.21965v2)). On a 2025 laptop CPU, even the "mobile" Segment Anything variant took about 24 s per image in segment-everything mode ([Ultralytics benchmarks](https://docs.ultralytics.com/models/mobile-sam)). A class-agnostic segmenter answers "where are the region boundaries", which dihedral angles already answer on a clean mesh. Learned sharp-edge detectors solve a problem you do not have; they exist because point clouds lack connectivity, and published detectors report edge errors of about 0.001–0.007 in unit-normalised space ([NerVE 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.16465)), worse than what the mesh edges give for free.

Models that write a CAD program from a shape are the headline research of 2025–2026 and the furthest from your hardware. CAD-Recode is a 2-billion-parameter language model released under CC-BY-NC ([CAD-Recode weights](https://huggingface.co/filapro/cad-recode-v1.5)). It sees 256 input points, needs ten sampled candidates to reach its reported accuracy, and its volume overlap falls **from 87.6% on its home benchmark to 64.3% on real scans** ([CAD-Recode](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.14042v1)). Extrapolating from generic small-language-model speeds, that is on the order of 10–20 minutes per part on a two-core i3; no CPU timing has been published, so that figure is an estimate. The best 2026 model scores 67 out of 100 on real mechanical parts and lists among its failure modes that countable features are approximated rather than counted ([CADENA 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.00799)). The commercial benchmark is Backflip's cloud model, trained on more than 100 million synthetic shapes ([3D Printing Industry](https://3dprintingindustry.com/news/backflip-ai-cuts-part-digitization-from-1500-to-10-with-new-cad-foundation-model-253596/)); an independent test found none of its candidates for a simple knob was without error, with a notch and a thin wall lost ([Engineering.com](https://www.engineering.com/backflips-back-is-the-mesh-to-cad-ai-real-this-time/)). These tools optimise overall resemblance. STL2STEP's rule is that holes and flats must be right.

Two learned options survive scrutiny, both as optional hint-givers whose output is always judged by a real fit and a tolerance test, so a wrong hint costs time and can never reach the STEP file. The first is a small "tabular" classifier such as a random forest or LightGBM, fed 20–40 hand-made numbers per region and used only to choose which primitive to try first. LightGBM's wheel is 1.4 MB ([PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/lightgbm/#files)), inference takes milliseconds, and a 2025 survey calls tree ensembles competitive, fast and deployable baselines on tabular CAD features ([survey 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.23719)). No paper tests this for surface types on meshes, and nothing was tried in the sandbox, so its accuracy is unknown. The second is a small image network labelling surface type on rendered views. In the sandbox a MobileNetV3-class segmenter took 225 ms per 512×512 view through ONNX Runtime, which adds only 14–24 MB ([ONNX Runtime on PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/onnxruntime/#files)); the researcher's estimate for an i3-7100-class chip is 0.3–0.7 s per view, so 5–15 s per part. It needs a training set, a GPU for training and several weeks, and it loses features narrower than a pixel, the same limit a large published multi-view system reports ([CADDreamer 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.20732v2)). No pretrained surface-type image model exists to shortcut that. Eight-bit quantisation is not a rescue on your chip: on an 8th-generation laptop CPU without the newer instructions it gave about 2× speed with a visible accuracy drop ([OpenVINO benchmark](https://github.com/mino19790622-dot/openvino-yolo-benchmark)).

Training data is a licence question in its own right. MFCAD++ is CC BY ([MFCAD++ record](https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/datasets/mfcad-dataset-dataset-for-paper-hierarchical-cadnet-learning-from/)). The Fusion 360 Gallery is restricted to non-commercial research, which is narrower than PolyForm Noncommercial's hobby use, and adds indemnity and terminate-at-will clauses ([Fusion 360 Gallery licence](https://github.com/AutodeskAILab/Fusion360GalleryDataset/blob/master/LICENSE.md)). ABC's models belong to their creators under Onshape's terms, with no warranty from the dataset authors ([ABC dataset](https://deep-geometry.github.io/abc-dataset/)). The safest source is shapes you generate yourself with OpenCASCADE and tessellate at several settings. Your hardpoint-STL format is a second source, as an inference: files that carry face labels are ready-made ground truth for a benchmark and ready-made training rows for a tabular classifier, and on such files the labelled faces need no detection at all.

## What to build, in order, and what to leave alone

The ranking weighs expected benefit against effort and risk for one hobbyist developer on the stated hardware. Size is a rough relative estimate, not a measurement. Every item sits behind the existing rule that a replacement is accepted only if it matches the full-resolution mesh within tolerance.

| Rank | Build | What it gives STL2STEP | Size | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Normal-histogram front end: merged exact normals with summed area; plane peaks plus offset histogram plus connected regions; plane removal, then axis-direction voting; each direction's facets passed to the existing 6×6 test | Deterministic plane detection without RANSAC; several lathe and extrusion axes per part for the Staged engine; the list of axes that every later step slices or views along | Small to medium | Published idea; sandbox timings; plane recipe is inference |
| 2 | Slab decomposition per discovered axis: levels from flat faces, one vector section per slab, line and arc fitting, global clustering of radii and centres, extrude when the two ends agree and loft when they do not; inner loops become pockets and holes | Exact prismatic rebuild from a few sections; a deterministic upgrade of Loft and Extrude outline | Medium | Open-source precedent (self-reported accuracy); sandbox section timings |
| 3 | Three-silhouette prism intersection as the first rough solid, with a volume-difference test against the mesh | A never-too-small first solid in two booleans; an instant "is this part outline-only?" answer | Small | Published recipe for drawings; mapping to meshes is inference |
| 4 | Solver-free snapping of fitted primitives: directions, square angles, refit with fixed direction, coaxial and coplanar, equal radii; hole patterns from clustered centres | One cylinder per hole and one plane per floor; fewer sliver faces | Small to medium | Inference from published methods; tolerances unknown |
| 5 | Ray-interval or depth-difference check of the STEP against the STL along three axes, plus the leftover map | Catches missed pockets and over-cuts, shows where, measures wall thickness, tolerates non-watertight input | Small to medium | Suggestion; sandbox cost only |
| 6 | Dihedral-angle histogram to set the sharp-edge threshold per file | A principled threshold in place of a fixed angle | Small | Inference to verify |
| 7 | Height map plus connected regions per axis, with a face-ID map back to the mesh | Pocket, boss and through-hole labels; a fallback for multi-shell or messy meshes | Small to medium | Sandbox on one easy part; no literature |
| 8 | Mirror-plane detection by pairs of matching primitives, confirmed by reflecting vertices | Halves the fitting work on symmetric parts; a consistency constraint | Medium | Inference; B-rep analogue is published |
| 9 | Experiment: tabular classifier that ranks which primitive to try, trained on self-generated shapes and hardpoint-labelled files | Possibly fewer wasted fit attempts | Medium (1–2 weeks, estimate) | Untested inference |
| 10 | Feature recognition on the output STEP as a quality report | Confirms holes are single cylinders; flags slivers | Small, if the BSD library integrates cleanly | Published |

Items 1 to 4 form one pipeline and should be built in that order, because the axes from item 1 drive items 2 and 3, and item 4 cleans what they produce. Item 5 is ranked fifth by dependence, not by importance: it needs output to check, but once the first four exist it becomes the tool that tells you whether each of them worked. Items 7 to 10 are optional and should wait for evidence from your own benchmark files that the first six leave a gap.

Leave the following alone. Hough or ellipse detection on rendered images as a way to measure anything, because a fit on vertices is exact and cheaper. Edge detection on normal maps, because it is the dihedral threshold with added blur. Multi-view rendering with label voting, because with exact face IDs there is nothing to vote on. Curvature and shape-index maps, because they fail on coarse CAD triangles. Geometry images, spin images and thickness-descriptor images. Adding more silhouette views, because no number of them recovers a blind pocket. Converting layered-depth data back to surfaces, because every published route yields a mesh, which you already have. Curvature-signature symmetry voting and GlobFit-style constrained optimisation, because they cost minutes and were built for scan noise. Zone graphs, program-synthesis CSG and prominent-cross-section clustering. Pretrained point-cloud segmenters, Segment-Anything lifting, learned edge detectors and CAD-writing language models, on grounds of CPU cost, licences and missing tolerance guarantees. Do not copy GPL code from CGAL or the kernel-Hough projects, or AGPL code; treat code with no licence file as unusable.

The honesty ledger has three columns. **Published evidence** supports the fingerprints of surfaces on the normal sphere, the need to remove planes before cylinder voting, the speed of clustered plane voting on scans, the unreliability of curvature on CAD meshes, the linear-time dihedral segmenter, the limits of silhouettes and single-surface-per-pixel images, the fragility and licences of learned models, and the existence of a working slab-decomposition converter. Most published timings are for laser scans on desktop CPUs, several table values were extracted by an automated reader and not cross-checked against the original PDFs, and some papers were readable only as abstracts. **Sandbox measurements** supply every millisecond figure for NumPy voting, OpenCV detectors, ray casting, sectioning and small-network inference, plus the 89.9% and 99.7% coverage figures. They were taken on a 2-vCPU cloud Xeon at 2.8 GHz with AVX-512, which an i3-7100 lacks, on one or two synthetic, axis-aligned, prismatic parts, with run-to-run noise of up to 4× on the heavier steps. No part with inclined features, fillets, draft or freeform surface was measured, and nothing was run on a 7th-generation i3. **Inference** covers the merged-normal plane detector, the ordering of the cylinder pipeline, the lathe-axis cue, the per-edge radius signal for fillets, the snapping sequence and its tolerances, primitive-pair symmetry voting, the mapping of drawing-reconstruction recipes onto meshes, render-based verification, the tabular classifier and the use of hardpoint labels. Each is reasoned from cited work and none has been benchmarked on CAD-exported meshes by anyone.

## Conclusion

The useful shift is in what "vision" means for this app. A camera pipeline detects in pixels because pixels are all it has. STL2STEP can run the same detectors on better raw material: the normal sphere is its image, sections are its contours, and connected regions over triangle adjacency are its blob labelling. Seen that way the classic converter, the Staged engine and the Extrude and Loft modes stop being separate engines. A direction histogram proposes the axes; the 6×6 test confirms what kind of feature sits on each; sections along those axes rebuild what is prismatic; region fitting takes the rest. Pixels return at the end, in the one role where being approximate is acceptable, as an independent witness that the STEP and the STL occupy the same space.

Two implications follow. Because almost none of the published numbers describe CAD-exported meshes on a low-end CPU, your own benchmark table on a named i3 would be new evidence and not just internal tooling, and it is the only way to settle the sandbox figures here. And since at least three small open-source projects now aim at "STL to STEP with real faces" ([stlToSolid](https://github.com/Crypto69/stlToSolid); [mesh2cad](https://github.com/Matthewjg95/mesh2cad); [unmesh](https://github.com/cmlarsen/unmesh)), one with the same licence and a very similar prismatic approach, the prismatic pipeline alone will not set STL2STEP apart. A measured deviation report for every conversion, and graceful handling of parts that are half prismatic and half organic, are where the difference will show.
