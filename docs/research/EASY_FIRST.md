# Research report: easy things first, then Guesstimate the rest

**WORK IN PROGRESS: MANY FEATURES MAY BE BROKEN** (STL2STEP 0.4.0-wip)

This is the full text of a research report produced for STL2STEP in October 2026 by an AI research agent (Claude) from papers, documentation, open-source projects and measurements made in a 2-core cloud sandbox (not on the project's target hardware). It was **not independently verified by the author** and is published so others can check it, argue with it and build on it. ## Claim the easy surfaces, then stop early

Doing the easy things first is the right order for STL2STEP, and the evidence says it costs nothing in accuracy as long as every claim passes two gates (a distance check and a normal-angle check) and the part is only declared "explained" when no triangle is left over, not merely when the claimed triangles fit. On the test meshes the payoff was concentrated and lopsided: routing turned parts to a lathe-first engine took the **coarse vase from 61.5 s to 3.2 s (282 faces to 5, max deviation 0.33 mm to 0.09 mm)** and the 24k-triangle vase from 264 s to 11 s, while sending a flat plate down the same staged path **doubled its time (7.8 s to 15.6 s)**, which is why the ladder must be driven by a front-end router rather than by a fixed order. Three millisecond-scale signals (a dihedral-angle histogram, exact-normal clusters whose boundaries are sharp, and a "do the normals meet the axis" lathe band) routed 25 of 26 meshes correctly for 9–720 ms each. Guesstimate on the organic leftover is a filler skip, not a tolerance trade: the blob went from 17.0 s to 9.5 s at 0.1, 0.3 and 1.0 mm alike, with max deviation rising from 0.059 to 0.106 mm, so the loose tolerance value should be fixed at a few times the gate and the time saving reported as what it is. Two real bugs surfaced in the app's own plane detector that no ordering fixes: cylinder facets of radius 10 mm or more are accepted as planes because the smooth-boundary fraction is counted per edge rather than per edge length (shaft 154 "planes", a 15 mm fillet split into 12–14 planes), and midpoint-subdividing the knob loses 44 of its 45 flats through a density-dependent "narrow strip" width estimate that no crease angle rescues. The caps that matter are on settling passes, per-patch fill time, the faceted fallback and a wall budget; `refine_rounds=1` saves nothing (76.4 s versus 79.0 s on the subdivided knob). The residual should stay a per-triangle label array, go to the kernel's own fillet when it is a constant-radius strip between two accepted primitives, to a one-shot linear B-spline fit at the loose tolerance when it is disc-like, and to planar facets otherwise, with exact and loose faces reported as two separate deviation numbers. Everything here is opt-in; the classic converter stays the default.

## Simple-first only fails when the gate is distance alone

The worry with "claim planes and cylinders before anything slow" is that a cheap test grabs something it should not: a plane claims a very flat spline face or the start of a big fillet, a cylinder claims a shallow cone, a vertical plane claims a drafted wall. The published record is consistent that this happens exactly when the acceptance test is a distance band and nothing else. The SHREC 2022 primitive-fitting benchmark found that "cones and tori" are "the most difficult primitive types to be recognized" across all six methods, that cylinders are misclassified as cones at roughly 7–17 % depending on method while planes are classified at better than 98 %, and that the methods which "fit all primitive families … then select the primitive with lowest error" produce false positives because large-radius cylinders or spheres approximate planes; one method had "11 outliers corresponding to the recognition of cones in which the estimated half-angle estimate is close to zero and the estimated vertex is far away from the origin", meaning a cylinder fitted as a degenerate cone with low fit error and nonsense parameters ([SHREC 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.07636)). Every system that keeps accuracy adds a second, normal-based test: Efficient RANSAC scores only points inside an ε band whose normals also deviate less than an angle α from the shape normal, with ε at 0.001–0.02 of the bounding box and α at 10°–35°, and keeps only the largest connected component so a plane cannot claim coplanar triangles across a gap ([Schnabel et al. 2007](https://www.hinkali.com/Education/PointCloud.pdf)); CGAL's region growing pairs a `maximum_distance` with a `maximum_angle` (20° for planes, 10° for circles and spheres in its examples) and documents that a stricter angle separates adjacent roof planes while a looser one merges them ([CGAL Shape Detection](https://doc.cgal.org/latest/Shape_detection/index.html)); Benkő, Martin and Várady run their decision hierarchy "in order of increasing complexity" from plane and sphere through translational and rotational surfaces, with each step accepted "within tolerance" ([Benkő, Martin, Várady 2001](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/1811/1/REalgorithms.pdf)).

The arithmetic says why the normal gate is the one that works, and it is worth having in plain numbers because it sets the gate values. Over a region of width w, a cylinder of radius R deviates from its chord plane by the sag w²/(8R), but its normals swing by about w/R radians. With a 0.05 mm distance tolerance and a 20 mm-wide region, **every cylinder with R above 1,000 mm passes the plane distance gate**; a 1° normal gate rejects the same region as a plane for any R below about 1,150 mm, and a 0.5° gate rejects R below 2,300 mm. The distance gate is quadratic in w/R and the normal gate is linear, so the normal gate is what catches large-radius fillets and nearly flat NURBS. The same arithmetic in the other direction shows the under-claim: a wall with 1° of draft is 0.35 mm off a vertical plane over a 20 mm height, so it fails a tight plane distance gate, and a 1° half-angle cone fitted as a cylinder has the same 0.35 mm per 20 mm error. This is derived, not measured; no paper reports a controlled "a plane was accepted for radius R at tolerance t" experiment. It does line up with the one published accuracy figure on exported meshes: Bénière's pipeline reports a maximum error of 0.275 mm "attributed to discretisation" after segmenting on dihedral angles first, which "significantly improves accuracy on sparse CAD meshes by isolating vertices from neighboring primitives" ([Bénière et al. 2013](https://www.academia.edu/102107004/A_comprehensive_process_of_reverse_engineering_from_3D_meshes_to_CAD_models)).

So the gate for each rung is three tests, not one. First, a one-sided distance from every mesh vertex in the region to the fitted surface, set near the numeric noise of on-surface vertices rather than at the mesher's deflection, because CAD exporters put vertices on the true surface and let the chords sag inside ([OCCT mesh guide](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/overview/html/occt_user_guides__mesh.html)). Second, a normal-angle test between each triangle normal and the surface normal at its projection, with the angle set at roughly half the exporter's own angular step so a tessellated cylinder is not split into planes. Third, a "beats the simpler type by more than the tolerance" rule when a more general type is tried after a simpler one, because a cone can always match a cylinder and a huge sphere can always match a plane; only this makes the type decision stable under tolerance changes, which is what a STEP exporter needs. Score-driven acceptance (RANSAC's inlier count, best-of-three error) picks the best-fitting model; tolerance-driven acceptance picks the simplest adequate one, and a plane must not become a 2,000 mm sphere because the sphere scored marginally better. Point2CAD applies exactly this rule per segment, fitting plane, sphere, cylinder and cone and selecting "the simplest model with the lowest reconstruction error" ([Point2CAD 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.04962)).

### The app's own plane detector is a worked example of the missing normal gate

The sandbox found two cases of over-claiming in `find_planes` that are precisely this failure, and they are worth fixing before any router exists. On a stepped shaft (radii 10, 7 and 4 mm with a cross hole) the classic engine found **154 "planes" covering 89 % of the area on the fine tessellation and 124 on the coarse one**, emitted the cylinders as facets, and on the fine mesh fell into the exact-faceted fallback after 23 s with 0 % exact surface. On an L-block with a 15 mm fillet between two planes it found 18–20 planes covering 100 %, the fillet being emitted as 12–14 planes. The instrumented cause is that `smooth_frac`, the share of a candidate region's boundary that is a smooth (sub-crease) edge, is counted per adjacency rather than per edge length: a two-triangle quad on a cylinder has two long smooth side adjacencies and two short sharp end adjacencies, which count-weighted is exactly 0.5, not above it, so the strip passes as a plane. Length-weighting the same fraction fixes it: **shaft fine 154 to 4 planes, shaft coarse 124 to 4, big-fillet 20 to 8 and 18 to 8, the filleted bracket 14 to 8**, unchanged on the bracket, vase, plate, tray, taper, blob and ovals. The cost is the knob, where the same change drops its 45 flats to 1, because the knob's grip is a 48-gon of 1.3 mm-wide flats whose long sides meet at 7.5° on both sides and are therefore "narrow strips with smooth sides" by construction. Those flats are genuinely ambiguous: on a 10 mm radius the sag of a 1.3 mm chord is 0.021 mm, inside the 0.0255 mm tolerance, so a cylinder would pass the distance gate there too. Whether a 48-gon prism should be kept as 48 planes or written as a cylinder is a product decision, and the normal gate is what makes it visible rather than accidental.

The second case is the subdivided-knob regression, which the earlier research flagged as a crease-angle problem and which turns out not to be one. The crease angle enters `find_planes` only as `max(crease_angle, 30°)` for the smooth-boundary fraction; fixing the crease at 10°, 21°, 29°, 30° or 60° leaves the midpoint-subdivided knob at 1 plane, as does doubling the tolerance or tightening the plane angle. All 1,791 rejections come from the narrow-strip rule, because the region width is estimated from the vertex covariance (3.46 × sqrt of the smallest eigenvalue): on the original four-vertex flat that estimate comes out at 3.46 mm against a true width of 1.3 mm and passes the 2.04 mm minimum; after subdivision the estimate drops to 1.96 mm and fails. A width that does not depend on vertex density (area divided by the major extent, or an oriented bounding rectangle) makes the two knobs agree. Loop subdivision is a separate matter: it moves vertices up to 0.121 mm off the surface, five times the tolerance, so Loop-subdivided flats are not planar within tolerance and should not be found; midpoint subdivision (`trimesh.remesh.subdivide`) is the right density stress test because it leaves geometry untouched.

## Shared edges come from intersecting the fitted surfaces, not from the mesh polyline

Once two neighbouring surfaces are claimed, the edge between them should be computed by intersecting the two fitted surfaces, and the traced mesh boundary should be kept as the check rather than the answer. Every B-rep-producing system found does it that way. Benkő's pipeline states that "in the case of a sharp edge … we just call a surface-surface intersection routine to compute it", computes vertices where three surfaces meet by curve-surface intersection, and handles vertices with more than three edges by ordering the intersections along the edge and inserting a small extra edge when the fattened topology contradicts the geometry ([Benkő, Martin, Várady 2001](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/1811/1/REalgorithms.pdf)). Bénière cuts the intersection curves at three-primitive junction vertices and assembles closed wires by weighted path-finding ([Bénière et al. 2013](https://www.academia.edu/102107004/A_comprehensive_process_of_reverse_engineering_from_3D_meshes_to_CAD_models)). Point2CAD trims each primitive "to form a margin of width ϵ around the supporting points to ensure enough space for the subsequent steps", then intersects the extended surfaces pairwise for edges and the edges for corners ([Point2CAD 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.04962)). The accuracy argument on a clean export is simple: a traced polyline's vertices lie on both true surfaces but its chords sag by up to the linear deflection, while an intersection curve is exact and inherits only the fit error of the two surfaces, which on exported vertices is at numeric-noise level. So intersection is both the standard and the more accurate choice, and the polyline becomes the gate: every polyline vertex should lie within tolerance of the computed curve, and if it does not, one of the two claims is wrong.

Tangent edges are the exception, and they are where a claim ladder silently loses accuracy. Benkő warns that "surface-surface intersections are likely to fail for smooth edges, where the adjacent surfaces are in principle tangential", and derives smooth edges from constrained fitting instead, "which guarantees that the smooth edge curve lies on both surfaces" ([Benkő, Martin, Várady 2001](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/1811/1/REalgorithms.pdf)); Point2CAD needs "strong regularization in extrapolation mode" to make the margins sane before intersecting. Both approaches fix the primary surfaces first and let the blend adapt, which is consistent with putting fillets after the surfaces they join and is an argument against ever claiming a fillet as a free cylinder or torus before its neighbours exist. The kernel detail that trips people up is sewing: `BRepBuilderAPI_Sewing` defaults to a tolerance of 1e-6, far tighter than any fitting residual, and in local-tolerance mode works at the sum of its own tolerance and the two edges' tolerances ([OCCT BRepBuilderAPI_Sewing](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_builder_a_p_i___sewing.html)). Faces built independently from intersected surfaces will not sew at that value; the robust route is to build both faces on the same `TopoDS_Edge` object so topology is shared and the residual is absorbed as edge tolerance, or to raise the sewing tolerance to the fit residual and no higher.

## Roughly 40 % of parts can exit at the extrusion rung

The early-exit question is how often a part is fully explained by planes, cylinders, extrusions and revolutions so that nothing slow ever runs. The firm numbers are about construction rather than surface type, but they point the same way. In the Fusion 360 Gallery, sketch and extrude are "the two most common CAD modeling operations used in 84 % and 79 % of designs", more than three times as prevalent as fillets and chamfers; the reconstruction subset of 8,625 sketch-and-extrude-only designs out of roughly 20,000 puts the purely extruded share at about **43 %**, and "line, circle, and arc represent 95 % of curves" in the sketches, with faces per design peaking at 5–10 because "simple washers and flat plates are common components" ([Fusion 360 Gallery](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.02392)). DeepCAD kept 178,238 of about a million ABC models by discarding anything beyond sketch and extrude, around 18 %, and found lines, arcs and circles "constitute 92 % of the cases" ([DeepCAD 2021](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2021/papers/Wu_DeepCAD_A_Deep_Generative_Network_for_Computer-Aided_Design_Models_ICCV_2021_paper.pdf)). A hard-coded set of 18 orthogonal extrusion-and-Boolean combinations covers "approximately 80 % of the DeepCAD dataset" ([Lambourne et al. 2022](https://damassets.autodesk.net/content/dam/autodesk/research/publications-assets/pdf/reconstructing-editable-prismatic-cad-from-rounded-voxel-models.pdf)). The fully analytic share (plane, cylinder, cone, sphere, torus and nothing else) is larger than the extrusion-only share, because revolves, fillets and chamfers add only analytic faces; the ABC paper's per-type histogram exists only as a figure, and the often-repeated claim that over 90 % of ABC surfaces are planes and cylinders comes from a student project page and is unverified ([meshcnn-4-cadseg](https://atomicsulfate.github.io/meshcnn-4-cadseg/)). What this means for the ladder is that the extrusion stage can end the whole run on a large minority of inputs for the price of one axis vote and a few sections, and that its section fits are almost always exact lines and arcs rather than splines; the 5–8 % of spline sections are where the shortcut should hand the face back to per-face fitting, as stlToSolid's B-spline-section-and-loft fallback does ([stlToSolid](https://github.com/Crypto69/stlToSolid)).

No paper reports "planes first cut runtime by X %"; the ablation does not exist. The indirect evidence is that a fully explained part is cheap: Schnabel's clean CAD fandisk of 12k points yielded 24 shapes in 0.57 s with 38 points unassigned, while the 1.9 M-point choir screen took 61.5 s and left 236k points, so time and residual accumulate together in the unexplained set ([Schnabel et al. 2007](https://www.hinkali.com/Education/PointCloud.pdf)). The accuracy risk of exiting early is not in the exit but in what the gate does not measure: a vertex-to-surface check cannot see under-coverage, a missed boss or hole inside a claimed face, so the exit condition must also require that every triangle is assigned (Schnabel's leftover count at zero, Benkő's no unclassified triangles), and the final verification should add a surface-to-mesh or volume check that is floored at the deflection. A part that passes both is reproduced to fit-noise level regardless of which rung produced it.

The extrusion shortcut has one accuracy lever that per-face fitting does not: the direction. An extrusion rebuilt from one section and one direction has a side-wall error of L·sin(δ) for direction error δ over length L. Benkő's measured-data benchmark had the translational direction come out 1.36° off while the rotational axis was only 0.17° off ([Benkő, Martin, Várady 2001](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/1811/1/REalgorithms.pdf)); 1.36° would be 2.4 mm over 100 mm. On a CAD export the direction must come from the planar end faces' normals or from many parallel edges, which are exact to numeric precision, not from a noisy fit. Done that way, the only direct number available is stlToSolid's servo bracket, 1,644 triangles to 23 faces at 0.060 mm maximum deviation against a 0.08 mm gate ([stlToSolid](https://github.com/Crypto69/stlToSolid)).

### Order dependence has cheap fixes

The documented ways a greedy ladder goes wrong are a wrong early shape stealing points, a region at a tangent or multi-edge vertex landing on the wrong face, and a more general type always being able to match a simpler one. The cheap published antidotes are largest-connected-component scoring and refitting on the final inlier set with the band widened to 3ε ([Schnabel et al. 2007](https://www.hinkali.com/Education/PointCloud.pdf)); growing all regions "simultaneously … in a reasonably even manner" and giving a contested triangle to "the closest region (assuming it is within tolerance)" rather than the first comer ([Benkő, Martin, Várady 2001](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/1811/1/REalgorithms.pdf)); and fit-all-types-keep-the-simplest-within-tolerance per segment ([Point2CAD 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.04962)). All of these are a handful of small least-squares solves per region, well within an i3; global set-cover or graph-cut selection over an over-complete candidate set is only needed when regions are not already confined, which is the point-cloud and scan case, not this one. The rule set that follows is: segment on sharp edges first so most regions are confined, test types simplest-first inside each region with both gates, accept a more complex type only when it beats the simpler one by more than the tolerance, refit with the type fixed, and hand back unclassified strips by closest surface. Removing claimed triangles from the pool before the next rung is safe exactly when claims are region-confined and normal-gated; it is unsafe on fillet-heavy castings where there is no sharp edge between a plane and its fillet, and the remedy there is Benkő's: strip high-curvature triangles out before claiming, claim the primaries, then return the strips by closest-surface fattening.

## Three millisecond signals route 25 of 26 test meshes

The router that decides which rungs to run needs to be cheaper than anything it skips, and it is: the full signal set (dihedral histogram, exact-normal clusters, axis tests, flat levels and vector sections) ran in **9–230 ms on meshes up to 20k triangles, 352 ms on the 24k vase and 720 ms on a 28k plate** where sections dominate, single-threaded in the sandbox. The earlier cost-model work measured the cube-map histogram alone at 0.1–3.3 ms and the axis vote at 0.1–1 ms on the same meshes. Against classic baselines of 0.4 s (bracket) to 264 s (vase) the router is noise everywhere except the bracket, where it is why sections and anything heavier must themselves be gated.

The dihedral-angle histogram turns out to carry three readable features on every CAD export: a zero peak of coplanar triangles (12–90 % of edge length), one or two "exporter step" spikes whose position tracks the tessellator's angular deflection (2.8° for OCC meshes at 0.1 rad, 9–10° at 0.35 rad, 5.8° on the bracket, 7.5° on the knob), and a sharp mass at 60–90°. OCCT's mesher documents that angular deflection "limits the angle between normals" at the nodes of each link ([OCCT mesh guide](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/overview/html/occt_user_guides__mesh.html)), and the measured spikes sit at about half the requested value. A valley rule (first bin after the smooth peak where the smoothed histogram falls below 2 % of the peak) landed at 3.8–15° on every mesh, always above the exporter spike and below the sharp mass, whereas the app's `_auto_crease` (three times the 90th percentile of sub-30° angles, clamped to 10–30°) lands at 10–30°. Otsu's threshold on the log-angle was unreliable (1.6–3.3° on lathe and organic meshes, where its two classes are the zero peak and the smooth peak rather than smooth versus sharp). No published method picks a mesh crease from the histogram automatically; a Bern thesis argues a single threshold "would need to be large enough to not consider edges in smooth curved regions and noisy regions but at the same time small enough to still find shallow features" and uses multi-criterion edge rating instead ([Seeholzer 2021](https://cgg.unibe.ch/media/resource_files/bachelor_thesis_lukas_seeholzer.pdf)). The valley is the better automatic estimate of the exporter step, with a 30° fallback when no smooth peak exists (the 12-triangle taper has none), but as the previous section showed, it does not fix the subdivided knob and should not be sold as doing so.

The exact-normal cluster signal separates prismatic parts from everything else once one correction is applied. Rounding normals to 1e-3 and clustering gives 6–8 paired peaks holding 65–100 % of the area on brackets, plates and blocks, and 0–2 peaks on turned or organic parts, which is Horn's Extended Gaussian Image in discrete form: a distribution concentrated on a few points is a polyhedron, one "nonzero only on a great circle" is the limit of cylinders, and one that depends only on latitude is a surface of revolution ([Horn 1984](https://people.csail.mit.edu/bkph/papers/EGI.pdf)). The raw peak count is fooled by a coarsely tessellated cylinder: the coarse shaft shows **30 "peaks" holding 76 % of the area at 10° steps**, and a rule based on peaks above 1 % routed it as prismatic. Requiring each cluster's boundary to be at least 50 % sharp by length (dihedral above 25°) removes the failure, taking the shaft's flat share from 0.76 to 0.15 while leaving true flats on brackets (0.91–0.95) and plates (0.89) untouched. This is the same information `find_planes` already computes as `smooth_frac`, length-weighted, which is why the two fixes are one fix.

The lathe band is the cheap discrete form of Pottmann's line-complex test, in which normal lines of a surface of revolution all meet the axis and "the pitch η of an approximating linear line complex … is nearly zero" ([Pottmann et al. 2002](https://www.isprs.org/proceedings/XXXIV/part3/papers/paper149.pdf)): for each candidate axis (the top three peak normals plus the three eigenvectors of the normal scatter matrix), solve for an axis point by least squares and measure the area whose normal has less than a chosen tangential component. At a 5° band the lathe share of curved area is **0.91–1.00 on every turned part (vase 0.99, shaft with cross hole 0.91, torus and sphere 1.00) and at most 0.09 on every prismatic or organic one**; a 2° band is tessellation-sensitive (knob 1.00 but the subdivided knob 0.79), because facet centroids of a k-gon sit up to half a facet angle off the radial direction. The earlier research's negative result on a simple angular ring statistic stands; the tangential-component test with a solved axis point is what works. Flat-level clustering along the dominant axis and mid-slab vector sections then confirm the prismatic route rather than decide it: sections at mid-slab heights through brackets, shafts and the vase come out as lines and arcs with residuals of 0.001–0.03 mm (coarse tessellation raises arc residuals tenfold, still below tolerance), while the blobs give one non-circular loop with a 4–4.5 mm circle residual and the spline-edged plates flag their outlines as "other".

The route rule that came out of this (prismatic if sharp-bounded flat area is at least 0.30 and flat plus perpendicular area is at least 0.75 and the lathe share of curved area is below 0.85; else lathe if that share is at least 0.85; else organic if flat area is below 0.05, lathe share below 0.5 and sharp edges under 2 % of edge length; else mixed) routed all 13 prismatic meshes to P, all 9 turned or round meshes to L including the knob, and both blobs to O. The one miss was the subdivided knob at the 2° band, routed mixed, which the 5° band fixes. The margins are comfortable on this set: P meshes have flat shares of 0.44–1.00 and lathe shares of 0.00–0.09; L meshes 0.91–1.00 lathe and at most 0.26 flat; O meshes 0 flat, about 0.1 lathe and under 1 % sharp.

| Signal | Threshold | Separates | Known failure or caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exact-normal cluster | round normals to 1e-3; cluster area ≥ 0.25 % | catches the knob's 1.3 mm flats (0.79 % each) that a 1 % cut misses | 48-gon flats are within tolerance of a cylinder, inherently ambiguous |
| Sharp boundary | length-weighted share of cluster boundary with dihedral > 25° ≥ 0.5 | removes coarse cylinder facets (shaft 0.76 → 0.15) | fillet-bounded true flats (knob) are "not sharp"; FreeCAD's 28.5° default angular deviation would put cylinder facets near 14° steps, still under 25°, unverified |
| Crease / exporter step | smooth peak = largest histogram peak < 30°; crease = first valley ≤ 2 % of peak (3.8–15° here); fallback 30° | on every mesh the valley sits above the exporter spike and below the sharp mass | does not fix the subdivided-knob regression |
| Lathe band | tangential normal component < sin 5°, not 2° | subdivided knob 0.79 → 1.00 | a k-gon prism with k ≥ 36 passes as a lathe; acceptable, it is within tolerance |
| Lathe route | lathe share of curved area ≥ 0.85 | L 0.91–1.00 vs P ≤ 0.09, O ≤ 0.11 | the shaft with a cross hole at 0.91 is the closest L; a lathe with big side flats (knob, 42 % flat) still routes L because flats are excluded |
| Prismatic route | flat_sharp ≥ 0.30 and flat_sharp + perp ≥ 0.75 | P ≥ 0.44 vs L ≤ 0.26 | drafted parts have zero perpendicular area about the main axis (the taper passes only via flat_sharp = 1.0) |
| Organic route | flat_sharp < 0.05, lathe share < 0.5, sharp length share < 2 % | blobs 0 / 0.1 / < 1 % | a scan of a prismatic part with rounded creases routes O or L, intended, since its flats are not planar within tolerance |

Two caveats bound the table. No real 3D scan was in the set, so thresholds for noisy meshes (a sharp mass from noise, a broken zero peak) are untested, and the app's stated input is clean CAD exports anyway. Mixed parts beyond the knob (a prismatic bracket with a turned boss, say) were not tested, so the M route is a fallback, not a validated class. Cube-map entropy (2.5–5 bits prismatic, 7–13 bits otherwise) adds nothing the peak and sharp-boundary measures do not already give and is not recommended as an input.

| knob_sub4 (11,832) | 79.0 s, 2,234 faces | 76.5 s, 8,462 faces | 57.3 s (organic 0.3) | both engines end in the faceted fallback; no route helps, the width rule does |

The vase rows are the strongest result in the whole study and the mechanism is plain: `find_planes` on a dense curved mesh is the single slowest segmentation step (8 s to find 16 % flats on the 24k vase), the primitive layout and NURBS passes then grind through surface that one revolve explains, and the staged engine's `rough.find_revolves` claims it in a second. A probe that ran the revolve finder on the whole mesh before `find_planes` claimed **81 % of the 24k vase in 1.45 s against 7.6 s for plane finding, 56 % of the knob in 0.27 s against 0.97 s, and 74 % of the coarse vase in 0.23 s against 1.05 s**, and found nothing on the blob in 0.33 s against 2.79 s; running lathe-first and passing the claimed triangles to `find_planes` as already labelled would cut a further 6 s or so from the vase's 11–15.6 s, an estimate that was not run end to end. The probe also exposed the shaft: `find_revolves` returns zero revolves on a stepped shaft at either tessellation, even restricted to the curved faces, while `find_extrudes` does find revolve pieces, so the lathe engine as written only handles single-profile bodies and the stepped-shaft case needs the revolve finder taught to accept piecewise profiles.

The plate row is the warning that keeps this from becoming "always run staged". The staged engine's extrusion path took twice as long as the classic engine on a flat plate with holes and produced a worse deviation (0.142 mm against 0.078 mm), and the guesstimate numbers on the knob show the same thing from the other side: the organic tolerance did nothing because the deviation lives in the facet regions. The pattern is consistent with the earlier cost model, in which each retry cycle through build-check-sew-repair costs 2.5–3.5 s and dominates CAD-like parts, so an engine that adds rungs on a part the classic engine already handles only adds cycles. The right reading of the table is that each route saves a lot on the parts it was built for and costs time on the rest, and the router's job is to never send a plate down the lathe path or a vase down the plane path.

## Caps on settling passes, fills and the faceted fallback matter more than refine_rounds

The subdivided knob is the part that shows which caps do anything. With the default `refine_rounds=3` the classic engine ran five NURBS passes (at 5.5, 25.9, 34.0, 37.8 and 40.9 s), built from 41 to 58.5 s, found the result not closed, and spent 58.8 to 77.0 s in the exact-faceted fallback, with the log reporting "23/13/8 patch(es) outside tolerance — refining", 14 faces failing the geometry check and 26 regions kept as facets. With `refine_rounds=1` it took **76.4 s against 79.0 s**, because that parameter only stops splitting after one pass while the loop keeps running "settling" passes up to `refine_rounds + 50`, on the code's comment that "settling always converges: every pass turns ≥1 patch into facets", and then still falls into the fallback. The earlier profile of the same run put `nurbs_face` at 37 s over 187 calls with a 7.7 s maximum, `nurbs_face_fill` at 30 s over 52 calls, `_build_faceted` at 15 s, `_shells_to_solid` at 12 s over three calls and 769 `BRepCheck_Analyzer` constructions at 9.8 s. The staged engine did no better: "99 region(s) kept as exact facets" cost 40 s of mending before 8,462 faces came out.

Five caps follow, and all are settable without touching the fitting code. A cap on settling passes (the `+ 50` is the uncapped part), a wall cap per plate fill of about 2 s after which the patch becomes a plain least-squares patch or a facet (one 7.7 s fill was observed, and the loose fitter in the next section makes this a demotion, not a loss), a cap on the build-and-mend stage so that three whole-shape `_shells_to_solid` calls do not run on a shape that already failed, an early call of the faceted fallback when Pass 1 shows NURBS covering more than about 90 % of a part whose histogram says it is a CAD part (0.4 % sharp edges and 63 % coplanar edges on the subdivided knob is a part whose flats were missed, and going straight to facets at about 5 s plus 17 s would have been roughly 22 s instead of 79, an estimate), and a wall budget of about 60 s on the i3 class after which unfinished freeform regions are demoted and the file is written. The faceted fallback itself is uncapped and is the most expensive single step on dense meshes at 17–26 s for 11.8k triangles, which is the reason the residual should be reduced before it is ever faceted. The one thing not to spend effort on is `refine_rounds`; the measurements say it is not the lever.

## The residual: fillet in the kernel, fit once loosely, or keep facets, and report two numbers

After the easy rungs there is a leftover: fillets and blends, freeform patches, and badly tessellated areas. The published pipelines all keep it the same way, as the set of points or faces not yet claimed, re-clustered by connectivity and re-run through recognition at the same tolerance. Romanengo's Hough pipeline discards points "close to the recognized primitive less than a threshold ε" (1–3 % of the bounding-box diagonal), clusters what remains with DBSCAN, re-applies recognition per cluster, and when the leftover is "sparse, then its points are returned as unclassified" ([Romanengo et al. 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0010448523000118)); Benkő and Várady send regions that fail every simple-surface test "to the next segmentation level through iterative subdivision until no further subdivision is needed" ([Benkő & Várady 2004](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010448503001593)); Point2CAD fits whatever is left with a small neural surface in "a few seconds on a single GPU" ([Point2CAD 2024](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Liu_Point2CAD_Reverse_Engineering_CAD_Models_from_3D_Point_Clouds_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf)). Nobody publishes a stop rule based on residual area or volume, so one is a reasonable engineering invention, not a cited practice. For STL2STEP the matching bookkeeping is a per-triangle label array (face id or −1), recluster the −1 set by connectivity with a small dihedral cut after every acceptance, and re-run only the cheap fits per cluster.

One thing the residual cannot be is a tessellated face inside the STEP. AP242 does define `TRIANGULATED_FACE` and OCCT reads it into a `TopoDS_Face` carrying only a `Poly_Triangulation`, but the STEP user guide documents a read switch (`read.step.tessellated`) and no write switch ([OCCT STEP guide](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/overview/html/occt_user_guides__step.html)), at least one open reader loads a NIST AP242 tessellated test file "with zero geometry" ([conway issue #521](https://github.com/bldrs-ai/conway/issues/521)), and a face with no analytic surface cannot take part in sewing or solid validity in the normal way. So "keep it as facets" means one planar `ADVANCED_FACE` per triangle, which is valid and sews, and which costs about **2.4 kB per face on disk (149 MB for 62,028 triangles, 9.4 s to build and 16.5 s to write) with read-back "explicitly problematic above ~20,000 faces"** ([mesh2step](https://github.com/tommasobbianchi/mesh2step)); FreeCAD is hit worst because its importer had a colour lookup "proportional to N²" in model size, fixed in a recent build ([FreeCAD issue #18735](https://github.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/issues/18735)). The budget rule that follows is a few thousand facet triangles per model, which means reducing the residual before faceting. With region boundaries locked and only vertex removal (no optimal placement) the surviving vertices stay exactly on the surface and the only new error is chordal, c²/(8R) for edge length c on curvature radius R, so an error budget ε₁ gives a curvature-adaptive edge cap of sqrt(8·R·ε₁): 2.4 mm at ε₁ = 0.15 mm and R = 5 mm. `ShapeUpgrade_UnifySameDomain` only merges faces "on coincident surfaces" within a `LinearTolerance` chord error and `AngularTolerance` ([OCCT UnifySameDomain](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_shape_upgrade___unify_same_domain.html)), so it cleans exactly coplanar facets but is not a near-coplanar merge; that has to be done on the mesh side by clustering normals within an angle, fitting one plane and checking its residual against the loose tolerance directly, which on a cylinder of radius R at 10° per facet is a sag of 0.0038·R, 0.038 mm at R = 10 mm, for 36 faces per turn.

Fillets are the one residual class with a cheap exact route, and it is the mirror image of the only modern mesh-fillet paper. DeFillet detects fillet strips by rolling-ball centres from a Voronoi diagram, removes them, and "formulate[s] the reconstruction of sharp features as a quadratic optimization problem" that extends the adjacent primary surfaces ([DeFillet 2025](https://xiaowuga.github.io/pub/DeFillet.html)); it is AGPL-3.0 and so unusable as a dependency ([DeFillet GitHub](https://github.com/xiaowuga/DeFillet)), but its half of the job is the half STL2STEP already does when it claims the primaries. The other half is the kernel's: `BRepFilletAPI_MakeFillet` takes a constant radius per edge, propagates automatically along tangent chains with `Add(E)`, and reports per-contour status (`StripeStatus`, `NbFaultyContours`, `HasResult` returning a shape with a hole when a corner fails); its documented limits are contour ends meeting "four or more edge intersections" and fillets overrunning the limiting face ([OCCT BRepFilletAPI_MakeFillet](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_fillet_a_p_i___make_fillet.html)), and practitioners add that multi-edge fillets are "significantly less robust than single-edge fillets in OCCT" and that adjacent fillets meeting at a tangent point fail ([cadshift](https://cadshift.com/blog/fillet-kernel-comparison-solidworks-inventor-freecad/)). The recipe is therefore: when a residual strip has two tangent neighbours that are both accepted primitives, the extended primitives intersect, and a few cross-sections perpendicular to the strip circle-fit to the same radius within tolerance, delete the strip from the residual, build the sharp solid, and call the fillet per edge with the status checked after each build. On a clean export the result is exact up to the radius estimate: the mid-fillet deviation is about dR·(1 − cos(θ/2)), 0.29·dR for a 90° fillet, so a 0.3 mm radius error is under 0.1 mm of surface error (derived, not measured). Failure is demotion, not retry: the strip goes back to the residual and takes the loose path, which is a local operation on an already valid sharp solid and never a whole-solid rebuild. Variable-radius and vertex blends are not worth attempting on this hardware; send them straight to the loose path. No paper benchmarks "intersect primaries then re-fillet" as a reverse-engineering strategy; it is a practitioner pattern.

For the freeform leftover the Guesstimate measurements settle what the loose tolerance is for. On the blob, 0.1, 0.3 and 1.0 mm all gave 9.4–9.5 s, 20 faces and 0.106–0.108 mm against the classic 17.0 s, 24 faces and 0.059 mm; the saving is the skipped plate filler and the tolerance value past about 0.1 mm buys nothing on this mesh. On the knob the three settings gave 10.1–10.5 s with the deviation unchanged at 0.129 mm and two regions still left as triangles against three. The implication is that a router can fix `organic_tol` at three or four times the gate without a user knob, and that regions left as triangles are a gate problem rather than a fitter problem: a loose tolerance only helps when the "is this cluster freeform-fittable" test (one boundary loop, parametrises to a disc, not too thin) is applied before the expensive attempt, and no tolerance rescues an annulus or a sliver. The cheapest honest fitter is the one OCCT ships: `GeomAPI_PointsToBSplineSurface` on a regular grid of samples, defaults DegMin 3, DegMax 8, C2, Tol3D 1e-3, plain least squares ([OCCT GeomAPI_PointsToBSplineSurface](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_geom_a_p_i___points_to_b_spline_surface.html)). The recipe is to parametrise the cluster to a rectangle, resample on an 8–20 per side grid, fit once at degree 3, measure the maximum vertex-to-surface distance on the original vertices rather than the grid, raise the grid density once if over tolerance, and demote to facets if still over. Nonlinear refinement is a second step only when the linear fit misses by a small margin: squared-distance minimisation converges in about 10–50 iterations where point-distance minimisation needs 100–400, but "foot point computation" is over 95 % of each iteration's cost ([Wang, Pottmann & Liu 2006](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/12/Fitting-B-spline-Curves-to-Point-Clouds-by-Curvature-Based-Squared-Distance-Minimization.pdf)). Boundary matching is not something any source does for free; the kernel route is to fit unconstrained, then build the trimmed face on the neighbours' existing `TopoDS_Edge`s so topology is shared and the gap is absorbed as edge tolerance, the same code path a planar facet face uses. Commercial tools make the same choice from the other end: QuickSurface's auto-surface is "a brute-force approach" that exposes patch count, not tolerance, and then checks deviation "against your supplied tolerance" afterwards ([DEVELOP3D](https://develop3d.com/reviews/review-kvs-quicksurface-1-0-3d-3d-scanning-cad-reverse-engineering/)), and Design X builds a patch network first and fits second ([Laser Design](https://www.laserdesign.com/4-easy-steps-to-nurbs-surface-modeling-in-geomagic-design-x/)).

The decision rule assembled from all of this, none of it a cited rule, is: a residual cluster goes to the kernel fillet when it is a constant-radius strip between two accepted primitives; to the loose B-spline fit when all analytic fits failed at the tight tolerance, it has one boundary loop and parametrises to a disc, and its area fits the remaining time budget; and to planar facets otherwise, including every cluster below a small triangle count, because a B-spline face would cost more STEP bytes than the triangles it replaces. Reporting then has to be split, because every validity check (closed shell, edge sharing, one-sided distance of exact faces) is per face and unaffected by loosening the organic tolerance, while the volume and Hausdorff totals are affected. Vendors report one colour map against one user tolerance: Design X's Accuracy Analyzer shows green inside the "user-defined ideal deviation" and red or blue outside ([Design X user guide](https://www.engineering.pitt.edu/contentassets/52314f399aba40fa86709314a569641c/geomagicdesignx2014userguide.pdf)), and Point2CAD reports a per-surface residual and a whole-model Chamfer separately ([Point2CAD 2024](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2024/papers/Liu_Point2CAD_Reverse_Engineering_CAD_Models_from_3D_Point_Clouds_CVPR_2024_paper.pdf)); none splits exact from loose. STL2STEP should: overall solid validity as a yes or no; exact faces with count and max and RMS vertex-to-face distance at the tight tolerance, which must be unchanged by any loose setting or the run is not "exact" and should say so; organic faces with count, area share and max and RMS at the loose tolerance; facet faces with count and area share and the reduction error ε₁ if the residual was reduced; and re-filleted edges with measured radius and kernel status. A single blended number such as "0.105 → 0.211 mm" hides that the exact faces did not move, and showing the unchanged exact number next to the loose one is the honest framing.

## Honesty ledger

**Published.** The simple-to-complex hierarchy and its justification, sharp edges by surface-surface intersection and smooth edges by constrained fitting, even region growth with closest-surface assignment, and the noisy-data accuracy figures (Benkő, Martin, Várady 2001; Benkő & Várady 2004); the ε-and-α double gate, largest-connected-component scoring and refit-on-inliers (Schnabel 2007; CGAL manual); the confusion rates and the "low fit error, wrong parameters" outliers (SHREC 2022); dihedral pre-segmentation improving accuracy and the 0.275 mm figure (Bénière 2013); the extrusion-only shares and sketch-curve mix (Fusion 360 Gallery; DeepCAD; Lambourne 2022); margin-then-intersect and simplest-within-error (Point2CAD); residual re-clustering and sparse-to-unclassified (Romanengo 2023); OCCT's sewing, fillet, B-spline fitting, unify and STEP tessellation documentation; the facet cost and read-back limits (mesh2step; FreeCAD issue #18735); DeFillet's method and AGPL licence; Horn's EGI and Pottmann's line-complex test; SDM versus PDM convergence (Wang, Pottmann & Liu 2006); vendor reporting (Design X, QuickSurface). Several sources were reachable only as abstracts or mirrors: the Bénière full text, Ke 2006, Attene 2006, Di Angelo 2019 and the DeFillet ACM page were not read, Benkő 2001 was read through the Cardiff repository copy, and the ">90 % planes and cylinders" ABC figure is a secondary, unverified claim. No paper ablates stage order or early exit on the same parts.

**Sandbox, not an i3.** Every timing, face count and deviation in the before/after table, the signal costs, the threshold margins, the lathe-first probe, the length-weighting results, the subdivided-knob diagnosis, the Guesstimate rows and the cap logs come from a 2-vCPU Intel Xeon container at 2.1 GHz with `OMP_NUM_THREADS=1`, Python with cadquery-ocp and trimesh as installed, over 26 meshes (12 existing test and scratchpad meshes, 12 generated with OCP at two tessellation densities, two synthetic organics), about 20 minutes of compute, with the repo read-only; the raw outputs were not kept with the repository. The earlier research estimated the i3 at 1.0–1.6 × these single-thread times and never measured it. No real scan was tested, no mixed part beyond the knob, and the staged engine's 2× on the plate and the filleted bracket's 0.598 mm deviation were observed and not profiled.

**Inference.** The over-claim arithmetic (sag versus normal swing, draft offsets), the fillet deviation formula, the chordal edge cap for residual reduction, the planar-merge sag table; the three-test gate as a rule set; the route thresholds beyond this mesh set; the five caps and their values; the "about 22 s instead of 79 s" early-fallback estimate and the "further 6 s" lathe-first estimate; the residual decision rule; the claim that regions left as triangles are a gate problem rather than a fitter problem; and the two-number report format. Each is reasoned from the cited work and the measurements, and none has been validated by changing the repo.

## Conclusion

The easy-first ladder is already most of what the classic converter does; what the measurements add is that its value is almost entirely in the rungs it lets you skip and in the work it lets you refuse, and that both depend on a router that knows the part type before anything expensive starts. The vase and the plate are the two halves of the same lesson: the same staged path is a 20× win on one and a 2× loss on the other, so "easy first" is not a fixed order but a per-part decision made in a few hundred milliseconds from the normals and the dihedral angles the export already carries. The accuracy side turned out to be a non-issue once the gate has a normal test and the exit requires every triangle to be spoken for; the two real accuracy failures found were in the app's own plane detector and were counting mistakes, not ordering mistakes.

Guesstimate's honest description is "skip the plate filler and say so". Its tolerance value barely matters on the meshes tested, its saving is real, and what it needs is not a looser number but a stricter admission test for which clusters get fitted at all, a kernel fillet for the strips that have an exact answer, a facet budget for the rest, and a report that shows the exact faces did not move. The next evidence that would change any of this is a `bench.csv` from the i3 with one row per route and per cap, and that is the only thing that should promote an opt-in to a default.
