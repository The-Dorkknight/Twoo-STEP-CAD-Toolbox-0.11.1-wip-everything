# Research report: combining the STL → STEP techniques

## Gate cheaply, fit the leftover, verify once

The thirteen techniques collapse into one ladder, and the ladder's job is mostly to stop work rather than to add it. The measured profile of the current code shows where the time goes: on the 2,958-triangle knob, **about half of the 15.5 s is the build/check/sew/repair loop that re-runs from scratch whenever any face fails**, segmentation is a quarter, and the NURBS fits themselves only an eighth; on the organic blob three quarters of 22.7 s is a primitive search that finds nothing plus plate fills; on the dense vase half of 15.6 s is plane detection for a part that is 84 % lathe; and a subdivided knob burned 60 s on fits that were thrown away before a 20 s faceted fallback. Against that, every proposed front-end stage (normal histogram, axis vote, sections, slab levels) costs 0.3–100 ms, so the question is never "can we afford the new stage" but "which expensive stage does it let us skip". The recommended pipeline is therefore: a sub-second always-on front end (dihedral histogram, Gauss-map histogram, axis vote, three-axis vector sections, volume) that routes the part down one of four paths (prismatic, lathe, organic, mixed); sharp-edge regions first and all global voting done per region; the 6×6 test and closed-form fits before any RANSAC; reduction and NURBS only on the residual that nothing exact explained; snapping after the gate with a re-gate; healing after assembly with its tolerance capped at the gate; one verification pass at the end. Three pairs genuinely conflict and are fixed by ordering alone (reduce before histograms or labels; snap after the final check; heal without a verification map); Extrude-outline/Loft is the same thing as slab decomposition with one slab and should be one implementation; the tabular classifier is redundant for every surface type except small tori and blends and is not worth its training data. On "vision after boundaries" the verdict is that the premise is right and the conclusion is wrong: once boundaries and axes exist as vertex data, rendering them and running pixel detectors is strictly slower and less accurate than running the same 2D algorithms on the projected boundary polylines (a per-hole window render costs 4–65 ms per feature and leaves sub-micron bias; classifying all six loops of a region from its vertices cost 2–60 ms and is exact). Pixels keep three narrow jobs: coarse occupancy grids for topology questions, a six-view visibility count, and the end-of-run STEP-vs-STL ray check. On the i3, two worker processes (not four, not threads) buy roughly 1.3× on fit-heavy parts; the retry caps buy more. All millisecond figures are from a 2-vCPU cloud Xeon, not your i3; the classic converter stays the default and everything here is opt-in until your own benchmark table says otherwise.

## Retries and doomed fits, not fitting, set today's run times

The combination question only makes sense against a measured cost profile, so the first thing to pin down is what the existing engines actually spend. Every number in this section is a sandbox measurement on a 2-vCPU Intel Xeon at 2.10 GHz with 8 GB and no GPU, Python 3.11, cadquery-ocp 8.0.1, trimesh 5.1, with Embree and Open3D present; profiled runs carry about 10 % cProfile overhead (knob: 15.5 s plain vs 17.2 s profiled). The i3 was never measured. The same Kaby/Skylake-class core at a similar clock suggests **1.0–1.6× the sandbox single-thread times** on the laptop, which is an unverified estimate, so read "15 s" as "15–25 s on your machine".

The knob (2,958 triangles, 45 planes plus fillets, 1,103 output faces, valid solid, 0.129 mm max deviation) is the representative CAD-like part. Its classic-engine time splits as: load and crease angle 0.03 s; sharp-edge plane segmentation (`find_planes`, `snap_to_planes`) 1.55 s; region layout with primitive RANSAC (`_layout_regions` → `grow_primitives`, 3,858 `_support` calls and 15,442 residual evaluations) 2.4 s; four NURBS passes over 25 fits 2.15 s; **build faces + checks + sew + solid + fix + mend, three full assemble cycles, 8.3 s (48 %)**; STEP write 0.43 s; STEP read-back 1.05 s; final check and deviation 0.7 s. The three cycles happened because two faces failed the geometry check at 7.3 s, one region failed to sew at 9.5 s, and each failure triggers `prepare()` plus `assemble()` over every face again at 2.5–3.5 s a cycle. Inside those cycles the cost is repetition rather than any single kernel call: 1,874 per-face `BRepCheck_Analyzer` constructions (2.7 s), nine `_sealed` tests (1.8 s), three `_shells_to_solid` (1.8 s), `_safe_unify` 1.5 s, 340 `_face_ok` calls (1.1 s), 233 `trimesh.closest_point` calls (0.9 s). Measured in isolation on the finished knob shape, the whole-shape `BRepCheck_Analyzer` is 0.21 s (0.15 s with the parallel flag), `ShapeFix_Shape` 0.54 s, `_shells_to_solid` 1.0 s, STEP read 1.0–1.2 s against 0.3 s to write. The staged engine on the same part took 13.0 s with the same shape of profile (two assemble cycles, 6.4 s). Python versus kernel: OCP builtins and constructors are about 52 % of the knob run, NumPy/SciPy 19 %, repo Python 21 %.

The other test meshes show the profile flipping with part type, which is the whole case for routing. The bracket (332 triangles, 6 planes and 3 cylinders) converts in 0.5–0.6 s, and **47 % of that is `measure_deviation`**, because `trimesh.closest_point` is the slow path ([trimesh issue #1116](https://github.com/mikedh/trimesh/issues/1116)); on trivial parts the verification is the cost. The vase (24,146 triangles, lathe) takes 15.6 s in the staged engine, of which **`find_planes` is 7.98 s (51 %) to find three flats covering 15.8 % of the area**, while `rough.find_revolves` recognised the three lathe surfaces covering 84 % in 0.99 s and built the 11 exact faces in 0.09 s; the remaining cost is the staged write/read-back `check()` trips (two STEP reads, 0.73 s), `fix_outlines` 0.6 s and a 1.4 s deviation check that meshes 14 revolve faces into 51,624 triangles. The blob (9,944 triangles, organic) takes 22.7 s: **`find_planes` 3.05 s to find zero planes, `grow_primitives` 7.25 s searching for cylinders and spheres that do not exist**, NURBS passes 10.3 s of which four plate fills are 7.9 s (one fill 3.4 s), build and verify 1.6 s. The knob Loop-subdivided once (11,832 triangles) is the worst case: the subdivision shifted the auto crease angle to 21°, plane detection found one flat instead of 45, the run went down the freeform path for 36.6 s of patch fitting (52 fills, one 7.7 s), 18.7 s of build/mend with 26 regions demoted, failed to close, and fell back to an exact faceted solid for another 19.5 s, **84.7 s total of which about 60 s produced nothing that reached the file**. Memory is a non-issue at these sizes: the process is 329 MB before any work because importing OCP costs about 210 MB, and working sets were +25 MB (bracket) to +246 MB (subdivided knob, during repeated whole-shape checks on a 2,234-face shape).

The published per-stage breakdowns agree on the shape of this profile. Le and Duan's CAD-mesh segmenter on an i5 spends 0.08–0.68 s on planes but 20–278 s on cylinder/cone/sphere/torus detection and 4–113 s on boundary refinement, and they note cost "depends on complexity of the model rather than its size" ([Le & Duan 2017](https://duanye.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/CAGD-2017-compressed.pdf)); the bracket-versus-knob ratio here (×25 time for ×9 triangles) says the same. The practical reading is that any new stage under 50 ms is noise and the only ways to save time are to avoid assemble/mend cycles, avoid `find_planes` and `grow_primitives` on regions a cheaper test already classified, avoid plate fills on patches that will be demoted, and never let a long doomed fit precede the faceted fallback. The measured cost of the proposed cheap stages on the same meshes is in the table; the sum of a histogram, axis vote, three-axis sections and three 256² ray-interval checks is 0.2–0.5 s, which is 2–5 % of a knob run and less than one retry cycle it could prevent, but 50–130 % of a bracket run, which is why the renders and ray checks must be gated too.

The gate signals those cheap stages produce separate the four test parts cleanly. Fraction of area in normal-histogram cells above 1 %: **bracket 0.91 (six peaks), knob 0.07, vase 0.16, blob 0.00**. Axis vote: bracket 0.49 flat to z and 0.51 perpendicular, knob 0.07 and 0.42, blob everything below 0.06. ## The techniques form one ladder, with three real conflicts

The literature settles the macro-order and it is the order the classic converter already follows. Every classic reverse-engineering system runs decimate/clean → segment with sharp edges and blends cut out first → fit simple analytic surfaces in a fixed hierarchy from plane to freeform → build a region adjacency graph → detect and enforce constraints → create the B-rep → add blends last; Benkő, Martin and Várady justify the hierarchy with the observation that "simpler surfaces (i) occur more frequently in mechanical engineering, and (ii) can be detected in a more reliable and efficient manner" and describe their direct segmentation as "an efficient, non-iterative approach" ([Benkő, Martin, Várady 2001](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/1811/1/REalgorithms.pdf)). Their 2004 paper runs the dimensionality tests (planar, ruled, doubly curved) on the Gauss map only inside regions already bounded by sharp edges ([Benkő & Várady 2004](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0010448503001593)), Bénière found that dihedral pre-segmentation before curvature classification "significantly improves accuracy on sparse CAD meshes" ([Bénière et al. 2013](https://www.academia.edu/102107004/A_comprehensive_process_of_reverse_engineering_from_3D_meshes_to_CAD_models)), and Le and Duan over-segment first, estimate major orientations from the normal poles and rings, remove the plane points, and only then search for circular primitives along those orientations ([Le & Duan 2017](https://duanye.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/CAGD-2017-compressed.pdf)). The surveys add that hybrid parts are the norm and that "adoption of multiple techniques within a single RE process usually represents the most convenient choice" ([Buonamici et al. 2018](https://flore.unifi.it/retrieve/e398c381-c4dd-179a-e053-3705fe0a4cff/CAD_15(3)_2018_443-464.pdf)). What no paper does is ablate pipeline order on the same parts; the ordering evidence is structural, repeated across every system, rather than a measured head-to-head.

The table below rates every pair that matters. S means one makes the other cheaper, more accurate or unnecessary; D means the same information twice (safe, wasteful); C means one degrades the other's input or their outputs disagree. Letters: A sharp-edge region growing, B RANSAC/NURBS/exact-facet with the tolerance gate, C the Staged engine (6×6 eigen-test, faces in surface coordinates, write/read-back repair), D Extrude-outline and Loft, E Guesstimate reduction, F normal-sphere histogram and axis/circle voting, G slab decomposition, H three-silhouette prism intersection, I solver-free snapping and symmetry, J rendered height/face-ID maps and ray-interval verification, K hardpoint-STL labels, L Jetwash healing, M the tabular classifier.

Three patterns run through the table. First, almost every "conflict" is an ordering problem and disappears once the rule "each stage may only consume slack the previous stage left" is followed: reduction after exact normals and labels have been consumed, snapping after the gate and re-gated, healing after the gate and capped at it. Second, the synergies cluster around one data structure. The region adjacency graph that A builds is what C annotates with type and axis, B with surface and deviation, I with relation arcs, J with per-node unexplained area, and K pre-seeds; Benkő calls it the thing that "reflects the complete topology and serves as the basis for building the final B-rep model" ([Benkő, Martin, Várady 2001](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/1811/1/REalgorithms.pdf)), and the Dassault feature-tree patent builds exactly this graph before deriving sketches and sweeps from its loops ([US11100710B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11100710)). Build it once; share the face-adjacency matrix, the per-face normal and area arrays, one point-to-mesh distance structure on the full mesh, one raster set, and E's collapse forest. Third, the two pure duplicates (D inside G, M beside F/C) are not harmful but are maintenance you do not need.

The tolerance ladder follows from the same rule and is the single thing most likely to prevent ghost slivers. With τ the user's gate tolerance: A's angular threshold comes from the dihedral histogram's knee but never below the exporter's angular deflection, or a smooth cylinder splits into facets; the B/C fit gate is τ measured mesh-vertex to surface, because CAD meshers put vertices on the surface and let chords sag inward by the linear deflection ([OCCT mesh guide](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/overview/html/occt_user_guides__mesh.html)); I's hard limits are half the A angle and τ/2 in offset or radius, then re-gate at τ; sewing working tolerance is max(1e-6, τ/10) and never larger than the smallest edge J or K say is real, following OCCT's own "use as small working tolerance as possible"; ShapeFix MaxTolerance is τ and MinTolerance the smallest real edge; the STEP write precision equals the sewing tolerance. Le and Duan use one ε (0.5 % of the bounding-box diagonal) and one α (20°) across every stage for the same reason ([Le & Duan 2017](https://duanye.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/CAGD-2017-compressed.pdf)). A merge criterion looser than the gate (same type, nearly same axis) is snapping in disguise before the gate and is where over-merging comes from.

## One pipeline: four routes, hard caps, and a single verification pass

The recommended combined pipeline runs as an always-on front end, a routing decision, per-route work with caps, and one shared back end. It is a schedule inferred from the measured profile and from the "anytime" precedents (Progressive-X returns dominant instances first and stops when the bound on undiscovered instances falls below one ([Progressive-X 2019](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_ICCV_2019/papers/Barath_Progressive-X_Efficient_Anytime_Multi-Model_Fitting_Algorithm_ICCV_2019_paper.pdf)); CGAL's RANSAC terminates on a probability-of-missing criterion and documents that looser epsilon shortens runtime ([CGAL Shape Detection](https://doc.cgal.org/latest/Shape_detection/index.html)); Attene's hierarchical merge tree lets granularity be chosen afterwards by "a slider controlling the number of clusters" ([EfPiSoft](https://efpisoft.sourceforge.net/))); no published converter implements profile-dependent caps, so the specific thresholds are starting values for your benchmark.

**Stage 0, always, under 0.3 s steady state.** Load, merge duplicate vertices, compute the dihedral histogram and set the per-file sharp-edge threshold from its knee (`_auto_crease` already exists; the subdivided-knob regression is a warning that this threshold decides the whole run), the area-weighted normal histogram with exact normals merged by rounding, the axis vote, eight vector sections on each of the three dominant axes, the mesh volume, and the flat-face height levels. Outputs: peak area fraction, per-axis flat and perpendicular fractions, section loop counts and whether loops are lines and arcs, the candidate axes list. No rendering here: the bracket shows that even a 512² render would double a sub-second run, and the silhouettes H needs come from Extrude-outline's vector traces.

**Stage 1, route.** Peak area fraction ≥ 0.8 and sections made of lines and arcs (bracket 0.91): the **prismatic route**, planes plus slab decomposition (D folded in: extrude where a slab's two ends agree, loft where they do not, global clustering of radii and centres across slabs so a hole is one cylinder), skip `grow_primitives` except on the residual, skip NURBS, one final check; expected 0.5–1 s and a bracket-class part drops to roughly 0.3 s once the read-back is skipped. A positive lathe test on a candidate axis (vase): the **lathe route**, roughing first via `rough.find_swept` (0.2–1 s), then `find_planes` only on the residual 16 % rather than spending 8 s on the full mesh. Peak fraction below 0.1, no axis signal, no sharp edges (blob): the **organic route**, skip `find_planes` and `grow_primitives` outright (−10 s of 22.7 s), decimate the whole residual with locked borders, fit NURBS at the organic tolerance with the fill caps below. Anything else (knob): the **mixed route**, which is today's order with caps: A → per-region global F on region normals → C eigen-test per region → closed-form fit for the named type → RANSAC only where the eigen ratios are inconclusive → explained-area check → E and NURBS on the residual. If hardpoint labels are present, skip A's extraction and the type votes on every route but keep everything downstream.

**Stage 2, early exit.** After the prismatic or lathe rough solid exists, compute its volume (`_volume`, 0.05–0.08 s) against the mesh volume; if |ΔV|/V < 0.5 % and the sampled vertex distance passes, write and stop. The three-silhouette prism intersection belongs here as the cheapest never-too-small container when the part has no interior loops in any section; silhouette intersection cannot recover concavities hidden from every view ([Slabaugh et al. 2001](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~allen/PHOTOPAPERS/SlabaughSurveyVolum.pdf)), so its volume difference against the mesh is exactly the list of what slab decomposition still has to cut. The stlToSolid converter already runs this axis-discovery → slab → line/arc fit → analytic rebuild → deviation-gate sequence and reports a servo bracket going from 1,644 triangles to 23 faces at 0.060 mm, author's own figures, under PolyForm Noncommercial, which makes it readable but not copyable without binding you to its terms ([stlToSolid](https://github.com/Crypto69/stlToSolid)).

**Stage 3, fit the residual with caps.** Decide demotion on the residual area fraction before any plate fill, not per patch after a 3–8 s fill. On Potato (your i3 and the 8 GB machines, which `perf.py` already classifies as potato because RAM < 12 GB or fewer than 4 physical cores): `refine_rounds` 1 (the subdivided knob ran five passes for 36 s before failing), at most 10 plate fills with a 2 s wall cap per fill (a 7.7 s fill was observed) after which the patch becomes an LSQ patch or facet; Coons fill for four-sided holes before any plate solve, as the earlier improvements report set out. Moderate (8th-gen i5 or six-core): 2 rounds, fills in a 2-process pool.

**Stage 4, snap, assemble, heal.** Snap in GlobFit's order (orientation, placement, equality) but by clustering and area-weighted averaging rather than optimisation, since GlobFit's constrained solve took 227–875 s per model fighting scanner noise ([GlobFit 2011](https://vecg.cs.ucl.ac.uk/Projects/SmartGeometry/globFit/paper_docs/globFit_sigg11.pdf)) and Benkő warns that sequential constraining "leads to an uneven error distribution" ([Benkő et al. 2002](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/1812/1/ConstrainedFitting.pdf)); snap each cluster to its mean, not to its first member, re-gate every snapped face, and only then derive edges by intersecting the snapped surfaces (the Staged engine's surface-coordinate trimming is the right tool: `Builder.prim_face` built the vase's 11 exact faces in 0.09 s total versus 95–300 ms per NURBS patch). Hole patterns and mirror planes come after snapping, from the fitted primitives, as hints for grouping rather than constraints. Assemble once; on failure rebuild only the demoted face's neighbourhood, with one extra cycle allowed on Potato and two on Moderate, instead of today's up-to-eight full rebuilds. Cap the staged `check()` read-back trips at one on Potato (each is 1.5–2 s on a knob-sized part) and skip read-back entirely when the written shape already passed `_sealed` and has fewer than 500 faces. Jetwash runs once here, after the gate, with MaxTolerance = τ and with J's unexplained map in hand so it never removes a face whose footprint still shows unexplained mesh.

**Stage 5, verify once.** A single whole-shape `BRepCheck_Analyzer` with the parallel flag (0.15 s versus 0.21 s on the knob), the sampled deviation on pooled samples rather than per-face `BRepMesh` (the vase's 1.4 s deviation is a Python node-copy loop, not kernel time), and the volume comparison. Add the three-axis 256² ray-interval check (0.06–0.11 s per axis plus a 1 s Embree warm-up the first time in a process) when the run exceeded about 5 s or faces exceed about 50, where its cost is amortised; on bracket-class parts it would be the biggest line item, so leave it opt-in there. Hard wall budget: about 60 s on Potato and 120 s on Moderate for inputs under 50k triangles; when exceeded, demote unfinished freeform regions to facets and finish, and never start the faceted fallback on more than about 20k triangles without the coplanar merge already applied, since that fallback costs 0.4–0.9 ms per triangle here and unmesh reports 0.46 ms per triangle for the same design ([unmesh issue #42](https://github.com/cmlarsen/unmesh/issues/42)).

Tied to the measured profile, the time accounting looks like this. The front end adds 0.01–0.1 s to every run. The prismatic route saves `grow_primitives` (up to 2.4 s on knob-class parts) and all NURBS work; the lathe route saves roughly 7 s of the vase's 8 s plane search; the organic route saves 10 s of the blob's 22.7 s; the fill and round caps would have stopped the subdivided knob at about 20 s instead of 85 s; the neighbourhood-only rebuild would roughly halve the knob's 8.3 s assemble block; skipping read-back saves 0.4–1.0 s on knob-sized parts and takes the bracket from 0.6 s to about 0.3 s. Nothing proposed here adds more than a second to any run, and the two items that could (1024² renders at 0.4–0.6 s each, native trimesh ray casting at 1.7–4.7 s and +350 MB) are excluded on Potato. Instrument it: `tools/bench.py` already logs stage markers and `exact_area_pct`; adding the explained-area figure after roughing and after primitives, before NURBS, makes every gate observable in `bench.csv`.

## Vision after boundaries: right premise, wrong conclusion

Your idea is that machine vision becomes cheap once earlier techniques have found the region boundaries and axes, because the vision can then be run on a small, well-posed window at a resolution chosen from the feature size. The premise is correct and is the organising principle of the whole pipeline: everything after the boundaries exist is cheap. The conclusion is wrong because the boundaries, once found, are already better input than any picture of them. The published systems that solve the exact problems "vision after boundaries" wants to solve all work on curves, not pixels: loop-based recognition of holes, pockets and bosses on B-rep models classifies an inner loop as depression or protrusion from the sign of one dot product and decides hole-versus-pocket from "loop shape itself (circular vs. irregular)", runs in 0.19–2.3 s on 103–1,275-face models, and beats a commercial recogniser 92 % to 68 % on bosses ([Lai et al. 2016](https://www.cad-journal.net/files/vol_13/CAD_13(1)_2016_95-107.pdf)); Analysis Situs recognises drilled, countersunk and counterbored holes by matching face groups against templates and looking for "circular loops in the parametric space" of the face, and warns that the intersection of two cylinders "is a Steinmetz curve, not a circle at all" ([Analysis Situs](https://analysissitus.org/features/features_recognize-drill-holes.html)); turned-part recognisers sort B-rep faces along the axis and apply rules, about 5 s per complex part ([Al-wswasi & Ivanov 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00170-019-03849-1)). No paper was found that re-renders an already-segmented region to classify it, in reverse engineering or in CAM feature recognition.

The sandbox test makes the cost and accuracy comparison concrete. On a 60 × 40 × 6 mm plate with four 3 mm holes as 64-gons, a hex pocket and a slot, with the top face given as the region and its inner loops as the features: extracting and classifying all six loops from the vertices cost **0.9 + 2.5 + 13 ms on the 342-triangle version (1.4 ms warm) and 29 + 57 + 1.4 ms on a 21,888-triangle version**, linear in region triangles and dominated by an avoidable `np.unique` call; the per-feature window render plus `findContours` plus circle fit cost **4–65 ms per hole** at 64²–512², independent of triangle count. Accuracy: the vertex loop fitted r = 1.500000 mm with zero centre error because the exporter put the vertices on the true circle; the best pixel window (512², 6.8 µm per pixel, 64 ms) gave r = 1.4989–1.5003 after the half-pixel correction, and `approxPolyDP` at 1.5 px turned the same true circle into 16–32 vertices, so the pixel route cannot distinguish a circle from a 16-gon while the vertex loop carries the exact 64 facets. Break-even on this cost model is about 25,000 region triangles per feature, which no clean CAD export produces for a plane with holes, and the boundary already exists from segmentation so its marginal cost is near zero. The "500 pixels across the feature" resolution rule cannot be applied per region either: at 6 µm per pixel the 60 mm face would be a 10,000² render, 400 MB of depth alone; it has to be per feature, which presupposes the features are isolated, at which point their loops are the cheaper input.

Two of the sub-ideas fail outright. Unrolling a cylinder into its (u, z) rectangle and running 2D tools there: a 4 mm cross hole in an R = 10 mm cylinder became a near-circle with 9 µm residual and a 0.2 % radius bias (the R·asin(y/R) stretch), which is above a 10 µm gate, and a hole straddling the ±π seam fitted as r = 30 mm; projecting the same boundary vertices onto the plane normal to the hole's own axis gave 0.5 µm residual with no seam problem, in 1.8 ms. The right 2D space for a cross hole is the hole's axis plane, not the host's parameter space. And classification by small render plus template matching cannot break the one hard degeneracy: the six vertices of a regular hexagon lie exactly on a circle, so both a vertex circle fit and a render see a 6-chord circle; what disambiguates for free is the adjacent 3D data, six planar walls with six distinct normals versus many facets whose normals sweep a circle. That is a case where vision after boundaries is strictly worse than looking at the wall normals.

What is actually best is to borrow the 2D algorithms and run them on the projected polylines. For a loop of N exact vertices (6 to a few hundred): project to the region plane or the fitted axis plane; compute the per-vertex turning angle and split at sign changes and jumps, which separates straight runs (turn exactly zero) from arcs (turn exactly constant) in O(N) without any significance test because there is no pixel noise; fit each piece with closed-form least squares (the algebraic circle fit returned exact centre and radius first pass, so RANSAC is unnecessary on a clean loop); then label the loop by its piece pattern: one arc = circle, two parallel equal lines plus two equal arcs = slot, four lines plus four equal arcs at right angles = rounded rectangle, n lines = n-gon (regular if equal sides, and a polygon rather than a circle when its chord sag far exceeds the exporter's evident tessellation tolerance), one line plus one arc = D-shape, otherwise spline. The sketch-recognition literature reaches 97–99 % primitive recognition on far noisier hand-drawn strokes with exactly these geometric features and no pixels ([Paulson et al. 2008](https://psi.engr.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/paulson2008no.pdf)); OpenCV exposes the Teh–Chin dominant-point approximations, `approxPolyDP`, three ellipse fitters and `matchShapes` as point-array functions that run on vertex loops without an image; the $1/$P/$Q template recognisers are BSD and about 100 lines for the odd named outlines (keyhole, double-D) ([$-family](https://depts.washington.edu/acelab/proj/dollar/index.html)); trimesh already ships `simplify_basic` and `fit_circle_check` ([trimesh path docs](https://trimesh.org/trimesh.path.simplify.html)). Region-type classification and fit seeding likewise come from what the segmentation stage already holds: the area-weighted covariance of a region's normals has rank 0 for a plane, one near-zero eigenvalue (the axis) for a cylinder, small-circle structure for a cone and isotropic spread for a sphere, one 3×3 eigen-solve per region, which is the C test's little brother and cannot be improved on by a render. Hole patterns are O(k²) tests on a dozen centres; the one shipping tool found does exactly that, bolt circles from three or more identical equally spaced holes and linear arrays from constant pitch ([build123d-mcp](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/pzfreo/build123d-mcp/tools/find_hole_patterns)), and its author's own bug report shows the standard ambiguity to handle (a square pattern reported as a bolt circle) ([draftwright issue](https://github.com/pzfreo/draftwright/issues/1611)).

The pixel grid keeps three jobs, and these are the honest residual value of image space after boundaries. Questions that are super-linear in the number of elements rather than triangles (which inner loop belongs to which face, whether a residual patch overlaps a feature, whether a fillet residual forms a closed ring around a hole or a strip along an edge) are O(elements²) on vectors but O(pixels) on a coarse grid; a 128²–256² occupancy grid built with `cv2.fillPoly` from the projected loops, no ray casting, costs 0.1–1 ms and is tolerance-free because it answers topology, not dimensions. A six-direction 128² face-ID render (60–100 ms once the BVH exists) gives the fraction of surface hidden from the axis views, which tells the router whether sections are needed instead of silhouettes; in the earlier sandbox six axis views saw only 89.9 % of a test part's area and the unseen tenth was the bore and pocket walls. And the end-of-run depth or ray-interval differencing between the tessellated STEP and the STL, which has no published precedent but is two-sided, localised and tolerant of open meshes, is the independent witness that the volume check and the one-sided vertex distance cannot be. Scanned or decimated meshes, where vertices no longer lie on the design surface, would change this verdict; they are outside the app's stated input and were not tested.

## Two spawned workers, not threads, and the algorithmic caps matter more

Parallelism on the i3 is worth about a third, and only in one form. The OCP binding does not release the GIL, so Python threads cannot overlap kernel work ([CadQuery group](https://groups.google.com/g/cadquery/c/zaJ-hUKwKmY)), and BLAS thread counts do nothing for this pipeline: the knob converted in 15.46 s with `OMP_NUM_THREADS=1` and 16.15 s with 2, so `perf.py`'s thread knob is irrelevant. Two independent STL2STEP processes on the 2-vCPU box ran in 17.4 s wall against 15.5 s for one, 31.7 s of user time, so independent process-level work scales about 1.9× across two cores. The i3 has two physical cores and four hyper-threads; a third and fourth worker would add perhaps 20–30 % throughput while mostly contending for the two cores, and each OCP-bearing process costs about 330 MB before data, so **two workers plus the parent (about 1 GB floor) is the ceiling on an 8 GB machine** and four workers belong on the six-core 16 GB laptop. Use the spawn start method and never fork after an OCCT parallel call, which inherits a thread pool without its threads and blocks forever ([adacpp PR #58](https://github.com/Krande/adacpp/pull/58)).

What parallelises with near-2× gain is per-region and per-axis work: `grow_primitives` candidates per smooth component (7 s on the blob, 2.4 s on the knob), `Builder.nurbs_face` per region (9.6 s blob, 37 s on the subdivided knob, with modest +15–30 MB per fill), per-axis sections, renders and ray-interval checks, and per-face validity and deviation checks if faces are shipped as BRep strings, which cost 0.1–0.2 ms and 3–4 kB for planar and small NURBS faces but 40 ms and 1.5 MB for a lathe face with a dense profile, so swept faces stay in the parent and only freeform patches and planar faces travel. What stays serial is `find_planes` as written (region growing over one label array), chain building and smoothing, sewing, `_shells_to_solid`, `ShapeFix_Shape`, `UnifySameDomain`, STEP write and read, and the final deviation after the shape is meshed. OCCT's in-kernel parallelism is a separate, cheaper lever: Booleans with `SetRunParallel` gave 1.2–1.6× on two vCPUs here and the OCCT developers report 2.24× average on four cores ([OCCT forum](https://occt3d.com/dev/content/parallelizing-bops/index.html)), the parallel `BRepCheck_Analyzer` flag gave 1.3–1.6× on whole-shape checks, and `BRepMesh_IncrementalMesh` is currently called with `isInParallel=False` in `shape_to_mesh`. The estimated gain on the knob with two workers is fits and primitive search 4.5 s → 2.5 s and the per-face checks inside assemble 4 s → 2.2 s, with a serial remainder of about 7 s, so **roughly 12 s instead of 15.5 s (1.3×)**; it was not measured because it needs `nurbs_face` refactored into a pure function. The caps on retry cycles and doomed fits are worth more than that on every mesh tested, and they are algorithmic, which is why they come first in the plan.

## What is published, what is sandbox, what is guessed

The reader deserves three columns. **Published** supports: the segment-then-fit-simple-to-complex macro-order and its justification (Benkő/Várady 2001 and 2004; Buonamici 2018); plane removal before cylinder-axis voting and the "flats swamp the axis" trap (Rabbani 2005; Le & Duan 2017); the slippage/line-geometry eigen-test and its eigenvalue table (Pottmann 2004); per-stage cost shape with planes negligible and curved-primitive detection dominant (Le & Duan 2017); greedy detect-remove-repeat and its order-dependence failure plus the three published antidotes (Schnabel 2007; Le & Duan's set cover; Romanengo 2023's re-recognise-the-residual loop); fit-then-constrain with dropped relations (GlobFit) and the warning against sequential constraining (Benkő 2002); the silhouette-intersection limit on hidden concavities; OCCT's own tolerance rules for sewing, healing and Booleans; loop-based and template-based feature recognition on curves rather than pixels (Lai 2016; Analysis Situs); sketch-primitive recognition rates on point sequences (Paulson 2008); and the ParSeNet/HPNet brittleness and licence problems that keep learned models out. Several of those PDFs were reachable only as abstracts or mirrors (GlobFit's staging is recalled, not re-read; Rosin & West and Teh & Chin were not readable in full), and no paper ablates pipeline order on the same parts.

**Sandbox** supplies every stage timing and memory figure in this report, from a 2-vCPU Intel Xeon at 2.10 GHz with AVX-512 and 8 GB, profiled with about 10 % overhead, on five meshes between 332 and 47,328 triangles and within a five-minute cap, so the superlinear behaviour of `find_planes` beyond 25k triangles is extrapolated; the subdivided-knob regression (1 plane found instead of 45) was observed and not diagnosed, so its ×5.5 scaling mixes a code-path change with size; the vision-after-boundaries comparison ran on one synthetic plate and one synthetic cylinder with six loops, in pure Python with an avoidable bottleneck on the vertex side; the simple ring statistic failed as a lathe gate; and no measurement was taken on the i3, an i5 or the app's C++ backend. The relative conclusions (pixels linear in resolution, vertices linear in triangles, vertex fits exact, retries dominate) do not depend on the hardware; the absolute seconds do.

**Inference** covers the four-route router and its thresholds (0.8, 0.1, 0.5 % volume, 60 s budget), the explained-area demotion rule, the neighbourhood-only rebuild, the read-back skip rule, the tolerance ladder, "reduce only the residual" (consistent with Schnabel's sequential removal and Fusion's organic-only reduction but literally implemented by nobody found), the three-check verification design, the Jetwash-with-unexplained-map rule, the per-region eigen test as a classifier that makes M redundant, and the 1.3× two-worker estimate. Each is reasoned from the cited work and the measurements, and none has been validated by implementing it in the repo, which was read-only for this research.

Licences constrain the build, not the ideas. CGAL's shape detection and plane regularisation are GPL ([CGAL reference](https://doc.cgal.org/5.0.3/Shape_detection/group__PkgShapeDetectionRef.html)), ELSDc is AGPL ([ELSDc](https://github.com/viorik/ELSDc)), DeFillet is AGPL, pymeshfix is GPL, and the DGtal implementation of linear-time arc segmentation is believed LGPL (unverified), so none of them can be absorbed; a turning-angle segmenter is about 50 lines and sidesteps the question. OpenCV (Apache-2.0), Open3D (MIT), embreex (BSD-3), trimesh (MIT), SciPy/NumPy (BSD), shapely (BSD) and the $-family recognisers (BSD) are all usable. stlToSolid is PolyForm Noncommercial like your app, which makes it a reference to read, not code to copy. Papers' algorithms are free to reimplement, and everything in the pipeline reduces to histograms, small eigen-solves, SciPy clustering and OCCT calls.

What is not worth doing, bluntly: the tabular classifier, because F and C already classify everything but small tori and blends and its training set costs more than one extra fit attempt per ambiguous region; per-region rendering, unrolling cylinders, and any pixel measurement, for the reasons above; a second implementation of Extrude-outline/Loft beside slab decomposition; global Gauss-map voting on the whole mesh for anything beyond the part frame when regions already exist; running the full-mesh gate on every hypothesis, which no published system does; 1024² renders, native trimesh ray casting, or more than two workers on the i3; GlobFit-style constrained optimisation; and any learned model on this hardware. Keep the classic converter as the default, expose each route and cap as an opt-in flag with its gate values logged, and let `bench.csv` on your own i3 decide which flags become defaults.

## Conclusion

The combination problem turns out to be a scheduling problem. The techniques are not competing engines but rungs on one claim ladder, and the measured profile says the ladder's value is in the rungs it lets you skip: a few milliseconds of counting normals and slicing decides whether a part needs 0.3 s or 20 s of work, and the retry loops that cost half of today's run time are replaced by decisions made once on the residual area. The region adjacency graph is the one object that makes this coherent; every stage reads it and annotates it, and every "conflict" in the matrix is a stage reading something a later stage has already altered.

The deeper implication for the vision idea is that STL2STEP's input is already the output that camera pipelines spend their effort reconstructing. Exact normals, exact adjacency and vertices on the design surface mean the "image" to analyse is the normal sphere, the section loops and the region graph, and the 2D algorithms from vision and sketch recognition apply to those directly, faster and without bias. Pixels return only where approximation is acceptable, as a topology sieve and as the final witness. Because nothing published describes this pipeline on CAD-exported meshes on a low-end CPU, the benchmark rows from your own i3, one per route and per cap, will be the first real evidence, and they are the only thing that should promote any of these opt-ins to default.
