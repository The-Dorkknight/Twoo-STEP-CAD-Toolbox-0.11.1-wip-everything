# Research report: potato safety and a time ceiling

**WORK IN PROGRESS: MANY FEATURES MAY BE BROKEN** (STL2STEP 0.4.0-wip)

This is the full text of a research report produced for STL2STEP in October 2026 by an AI research agent (Claude) from documentation, forum threads, papers and prototypes measured in a 2-core cloud sandbox (not on the project's target laptops). It was **not independently verified by the author** and is published so others can check it, argue with it and build on it. A plain-English summary with the status of what has been built is in [README.md](README.md). Sandbox measurements and inferences are labelled as such.

---

## Write a file early, then kill what runs late

A potato PC cannot be made safe by catching errors, because the errors that matter never reach Python: when OpenCASCADE runs out of memory inside a plate fill, Linux kills the whole process without warning and Windows lets it grind into the pagefile and then hang, and neither case raises a `MemoryError` the app can handle. The only design that holds up is the one now in the repo: **write a valid STEP before the dangerous stage starts, replace it atomically whenever a better one exists, and treat everything after the checkpoint as optional work that may die**. The same logic covers time. OpenCASCADE 7.5–7.8 can cooperatively cancel meshing, Booleans, sewing, plate surfaces and STEP transfer through a progress object, but not `BRepCheck_Analyzer`, `UnifySameDomain` or `ShapeFix`, and even the covered algorithms sometimes sit in code paths that never poll, so the only hard ceiling is a child process with `join(timeout)` and `kill()`. Scaling the sandbox numbers says the 45-minute i5 target is reachable for every test mesh: an 11th-gen i5-1135G7 benchmarks at about 1.9× an i3-7100U single-threaded and 3.4× multi-threaded, but sustained clocks on thin laptops sit at 3.1–3.2 GHz rather than the 4.2 GHz turbo, so plan on roughly half the sandbox time, with the i3 taking about the sandbox time or a little more. Of the measured prototypes, the face cache across assemble cycles is safe (bit-identical output once faces are deep-copied, 17–34 % fewer whole-shape checks, 0.5–2 s saved on 10–20 s parts) but not a big win, and the density-independent strip-width rule restores the subdivided knob's 45 flats on every mesh tested at the price of one 0.6 mm deviation case in Classic, which is why it ships gated to the Ladder engine. What was built from this (checkpoint on 20k+ triangles, exception-safe fallback to it, 2.5 h / 40 min profile budgets with plate-fill allowances, memory-tight skip of the filler, faceted-fallback reduction above 20k triangles, the face cache, the sagitta rule, a rough time estimate in the log) makes the app finish with a file in every case the sandbox could provoke. What remains (progressive organic fitting with repeated checkpoints, a two-process fit pool, a per-call child-process watchdog, and OpenCASCADE progress cancellation) is what turns "finishes with a file" into "finishes on time".

## A checkpoint is cheap to write and expensive only if it has too many faces

The "anytime" idea from the fitting literature is the right frame: Progressive-X describes an algorithm that works by "interleaving sampling and consolidation of the current data interpretation" so that "whenever [it] is interrupted, e.g. due to a time limit, the returned instances cover real and, likely, the most dominant ones" ([Barath & Matas 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1906.02290)). Translated to STL2STEP, the kept set is the recognised exact faces plus whatever is still facets, and it is valid after every accepted face. The app's checkpoint does exactly this once, at the point where the risk starts. In `_build_surface`, when the mesh has 20,000 or more triangles (or `checkpoint="on"`), every region typed `nurbs` is retyped `facet`, the shape is prepared, assembled, sealed, written to `<out>.part` and moved over the target with `os.replace`, and the log says "a usable file exists from here on" (`app/stl2step_core.py`). The atomic rename is the part people get wrong on Windows: `os.replace` is documented as replacing an existing file silently and as "an atomic operation (this is a POSIX requirement)", whereas `os.rename` raises `FileExistsError` on Windows when the target exists ([Python docs, os](https://docs.python.org/3/library/os.html#os.replace)). For durability across a power cut the docs add that you must "first do f.flush(), and then do os.fsync(f.fileno())" before the rename ([Python docs, os.fsync](https://docs.python.org/3/library/os.html#os.fsync)); the app now reopens the temporary file and calls `os.fsync` on it before the rename, so a power cut during the write leaves the previous file, not a half-written one.

The write itself is not the expensive part. The only published timing found is mesh2step's: a **62,028-triangle faceted B-rep took 9.4 s to build and 16.5 s to write, producing a 149 MB STEP**, and the same author reports that re-reading that file with OCCT's own `STEPControl_Reader` "had not completed after 300 s" ([mesh2step README](https://github.com/tommasobbianchi/mesh2step)). That is the ceiling that matters: a checkpoint whose leftover is tens of thousands of planar facets is a file nobody can open. Fusion 360 users hit the same wall a decade earlier with a 22 MB STEP converted from a 446 KB STL, where rotation "often caused Fusion 360 to crash" and Autodesk support's explanation was simply "More Faces = More Edges which could help slow everything down" ([Autodesk Community](https://forums.autodesk.com/t5/fusion-design-validate-document/program-extremely-slow-and-crashy-with-stl-converted-to-stp/td-p/4363229)), and FreeCAD's importer had a colour lookup "proportional to N²" in the number of labels that turned a one-minute SolidWorks import into an hour ([FreeCAD issue #18735](https://github.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/issues/18735)). mesh2step's recommendation is a cap near 20,000 faces, reached by decimation or coplanar merge before any OCCT topology is built, and that is where the repo's `FACET_CAP = 20000` and the `reduce_within(ctx.m, tol / 2.0, …)` call in `_build_faceted` come from (`app/stl2step_core.py`). The merge has to happen on the mesh side because OCCT's `ShapeUpgrade_UnifySameDomain` compares faces pairwise with no spatial index and took 75–90 s on a 465-face shape ([pythonocc-core #1477](https://github.com/tpaviot/pythonocc-core/issues/1477)); on 20,000 faces it would never finish.

Two details from the OCCT documentation shape how a checkpoint should be written if the app ever writes more than one. `BRepTools::Write` and `BRepTools::Read` both take a `Message_ProgressRange`, so a native `.brep` checkpoint is itself cancellable, and `BRepTools::Clean` drops cached triangulation before serialising ([OCCT refman, BRepTools](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_tools.html)). Binary BRep is not automatically faster: a forum thread measured a 3.7 MB binary file loading in 48 s against 51 s for the ASCII version, with the real slowdown being nested location matrices rather than the format ([OCCT forum](https://occt3d.com/dev/content/brep-file-loading-very-slow/index.html)). And `STEPControl_Writer::Transfer` takes a progress range while `Write` does not, with `AsIs` mode mapping each sub-shape to "its highest possible STEP representation" ([OCCT refman, STEPControl_Writer](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_s_t_e_p_control___writer.html)), which is why writing a compound of a solid plus open shells is legal, though how FreeCAD imports such a mixed compound was not verified. The practical rule: write STEP for the user, keep the faceted leftover under 20,000 faces, and if a second checkpoint format is ever added, measure `.brep` versus `.step` on the real shapes rather than assuming.

## Out of memory is not an exception you can catch

The reason memory safety has to be structural is that the failure never arrives as a Python exception. On Linux the default overcommit mode means "obvious overcommits of address space are refused" but everything else is granted, and the kernel only discovers the lie when pages are touched; only mode 2, where "the total address space commit for the system is not permitted to exceed swap + a configurable amount (default is 50%) of physical RAM", makes allocations fail at `malloc` time ([kernel.org, overcommit-accounting](https://www.kernel.org/doc/html/latest/mm/overcommit-accounting.html)). Desktops run mode 0, so OpenCASCADE's `new` succeeds and the OOM killer sends SIGKILL later, and before that Linux desktops "typically freeze rather than immediately invoking the OOM killer" because executable pages get evicted and re-read in a loop ([Arch Linux forum](https://bbs.archlinux.org/viewtopic.php?id=233843)); Fedora ships earlyoom for this reason and 2026 bug reports still describe "nothing intervenes before a low-memory hard freeze" ([bazzite #5426](https://github.com/ublue-os/bazzite/issues/5426)). Windows is the opposite failure. The commit limit "is the sum of physical memory and the size of all of the page files combined", a system-managed pagefile grows "up to three times physical memory or 4 GB whichever is larger", and when commit is exhausted "applications may fail to function and the system may hang indefinitely" ([Microsoft, out of system committed memory](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/technet-wiki/2248.perfguide-out-of-system-committed-memory)). So an 8 GB Windows laptop lets a runaway plate fill grind through up to 24 GB of commit on the SSD before an allocation fails inside OCCT, and Windows never kills the process. Linux kills; Windows grinds then hangs. Neither hands Python a `MemoryError` at the point where the app could do something about it.

Even where an allocation does fail, catching it through the binding is unreliable. NumPy's maintainers, asked for a memory cap, reported that `RLIMIT_AS` and `RLIMIT_DATA` "caused failures far below the intended limit" because OpenBLAS thread stacks and mmap reservations consume address space independent of data, that `RLIMIT_RSS` "didn't enforce limits at all", and that `MemoryError` "isn't guaranteed when hitting limits"; their advice was cgroups or application-level monitoring ([numpy #26551](https://github.com/numpy/numpy/issues/26551)). Python's own docs describe `RLIMIT_RSS` as the resident set size "that should be made available", advisory wording that modern Linux ignores ([Python docs, resource](https://docs.python.org/3/library/resource.html)). Whether pybind11's default `std::bad_alloc` to `MemoryError` mapping survives OCCT's own `Standard_OutOfMemory` paths was not verified for OCP, and it does not matter much: the repo's `except Exception` around `_build_surface` is correct as far as it goes, since it catches whatever does surface, falls back to the checkpoint file if one was written, and otherwise builds the exact faceted solid (`app/stl2step_core.py`). What it cannot catch is the SIGKILL, and that is why the checkpoint on disk, not the handler, is the real safety net.

The limits that do work are the ones the operating system enforces on a process from outside. On Windows a Job Object with `JOB_OBJECT_LIMIT_PROCESS_MEMORY` "specifies the limit for the virtual memory that can be committed by a process", with a job-wide `JobMemoryLimit` and `PeakProcessMemoryUsed` for reporting ([Microsoft Learn, JOBOBJECT_EXTENDED_LIMIT_INFORMATION](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/api/winnt/ns-winnt-jobobject_extended_limit_information)); note that this caps commit, so breaching it makes the allocation fail inside OCCT rather than killing anything, and `JOB_OBJECT_LIMIT_KILL_ON_JOB_CLOSE` is the usual companion so orphans die with the parent. On Linux `RLIMIT_AS` works only with a margin of several gigabytes above expected RSS, for the reasons NumPy found, and with `OPENBLAS_NUM_THREADS=1` set in the child (the repo's `perf.worker_env` already sets the BLAS thread variables). The portable answer is polling: psutil, BSD-licensed, gives `Process.memory_info().rss`, `virtual_memory().available`, `wait(timeout)` and `kill()` ([psutil docs](https://psutil.io/)). The repo's `_memory_tight()` is the first use of this: it skips the plate filler when the worker's RSS exceeds 75 % of total RAM or fewer than 400 MB are available (`app/stl2step_core.py`). Blunt assessment of that threshold on the 4 GB i3: 75 % of total is 3 GB of RSS for a process that starts at about 330 MB after importing OCP, and a Windows machine with a 4 GB budget will already be paging long before then, so the `available < 400 MB` clause is the one that fires first and it fires late. A better soft cap is relative to what was available when the run started, around 60 %, and it belongs on the per-call child process described in the next section rather than on the worker that holds the whole mesh.

Which OpenCASCADE calls are the risky ones is known from bug reports rather than documentation; no OCCT source quantifies memory growth for any algorithm, so each has to be treated as unbounded and sandboxed. The plate filler, `BRepFill_Filling` over `GeomPlate_BuildPlateSurface`, is the worst: CadQuery users found that "the surface needs to be relatively simple, and the number of interpolation points limited, otherwise it crashes", that any continuity above C0 crashed, and settled on Degree 3, 15–20 points per curve, NbIter 2 ([CadQuery #229](https://github.com/CadQuery/cadquery/issues/229)); OCCT's own docs say `NbIter` "is used to control the duration of computation" and the `Perform` call takes a progress range ([OCCT refman, GeomPlate_BuildPlateSurface](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_geom_plate___build_plate_surface.html)). `ShapeUpgrade_UnifySameDomain` has no cache, compares all face pairs, and OCCT 8.0 fixed "a case where UnifySameDomain could loop with 100% CPU on periodic surfaces"; the knobs are a larger linear tolerance, an angular tolerance near 0.1 rad and `SetSafeInputMode(False)` ([pythonocc-core #1477](https://github.com/tpaviot/pythonocc-core/issues/1477), [XbimGeometry #483](https://github.com/xBimTeam/XbimGeometry/issues/483)). `BRepBuilderAPI_Sewing` is cancellable through `Perform(theProgress)` and its cost is super-linear in face count: mesh2step's shared-topology construction was 2.6–3.2× faster than sewing "with the gap widening super-linearly as triangle count grows" ([mesh2step](https://github.com/tommasobbianchi/mesh2step), [OCCT refman, Sewing](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_builder_a_p_i___sewing.html)). `BRepMesh` has `MinSize` to "prevent sinking into amplification in case of distorted curves and surfaces" and `InParallel` ([OCCT refman, IMeshTools_Parameters](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/struct_i_mesh_tools___parameters.html)), and the earlier profiling already found that the deviation check could reach 2.5 GB on long thin triangles until it was blocked, which is the one memory blow-up in this app that was actually observed. `BRepCheck_Analyzer` has a parallel flag and nothing else; it cannot be interrupted or bounded except by running it where it can be killed ([OCCT refman, BRepCheck_Analyzer](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_b_rep_check___analyzer.html)).

## Cooperative cancellation covers most of OCCT; a killable child covers all of it

OpenCASCADE 7.5 redesigned progress reporting so that an application creates a `Message_ProgressIndicator`, hands out `Message_ProgressRange` portions to nested algorithms, and algorithms poll for cancellation with `if (!aPScope.More()) return false;`, returning a flag rather than throwing ([Gavrilov, Progress Indication changes in OCCT 7.5.0](https://unlimited3d.wordpress.com/2020/10/17/progress-indication-changes-in-occt-7-5-0/)). The indicator's `UserBreak()` "should return True if user has sent a break signal", may be called from several threads and must be thread-safe ([OCCT refman, Message_ProgressIndicator](https://occt3d.com/dev/doc/refman/html/class_message___progress_indicator.html)). Coverage grew by version and is what cadquery-ocp 7.7/7.8 inherits: BRepMesh gained it in 7.5.0 ([OCCT 7.5.0 release](https://www.opencascade.com/open-cascade-technology-7-5-0-released/)), and 7.6.0 "added progress indication and user break support for Boolean operations, GeomPlate, DistShapeShape, Shape Offset" ([OCCT 7.6.0 release](https://www.opencascade.com/open-cascade-technology-7-6-0-released/)); sewing, `STEPControl_Writer::Transfer` and `BRepTools::Read/Write` carry the parameter in their signatures. Not covered: `BRepCheck_Analyzer`, `ShapeUpgrade_UnifySameDomain`, and `ShapeFix_Shape` (not individually verified, but no progress parameter was found). Even the covered ones are not a guarantee. FreeCAD's recent work to make Booleans abortable notes that "sometimes OCCT can be in a code path that doesn't check UserBreak() frequently enough", requires OCCT 7.8.1 or later, and warns that "thread cancellation can segfault FreeCAD" ([FreeCAD PR #19763](https://github.com/FreeCAD/FreeCAD/pull/19763)); the same author lists helix, pipe, loft, fillet and chamfer as uncovered and slow ([Stanley 2025](https://incoherency.co.uk/blog/stories/freecad-abort-long-running-operations.html)). A C++ consumer found every tessellation call it made was uncancellable simply because it passed the default `Message_ProgressRange()`, "so OCCT never asks whether to stop", and pinned 325 % CPU indefinitely until a progress sink was threaded into every call site ([Cadly #5](https://github.com/broccoli-97/Cadly/issues/5)). The app currently passes no progress object anywhere, so today it is in Cadly's starting position.

Whether the Python side can even supply one is unverified. OCP ships stubs for `Message_ProgressIndicator`, `Message_ProgressRange` and `Message_ProgressScope` ([CadQuery/OCP-stubs](https://github.com/CadQuery/OCP-stubs)), but pybind11 only lets a Python subclass override a virtual method when the binding declares a trampoline class ([pybind11 docs](https://pybind11-jagerman.readthedocs.io/en/latest/advanced/classes.html)), and whether OCP's binding of `Message_ProgressIndicator` has one could not be confirmed. The test is a ten-line script: subclass it, return `True` from `UserBreak()` after a deadline, pass `Start()` into `BRepMesh_IncrementalMesh(shape, params, range)`, and see whether the override is ever called. If it is, the Python override must also re-acquire the GIL on every poll, which adds latency but works. If it is not, the cooperative layer is unavailable from Python and only the hard layer remains.

The hard layer is a child process, and it is the only mechanism with no exceptions. `multiprocessing.Process.join(timeout)`, `terminate()` (SIGTERM, or `TerminateProcess()` on Windows) and `kill()` (SIGKILL) are documented, with the caveats that terminating a process using a pipe or queue "corrupts them", exit handlers and `finally` clauses do not run, and descendants are not terminated ([Python docs, multiprocessing](https://docs.python.org/3/library/multiprocessing.html)). The app already runs the conversion in a worker that the GUI can kill; what is missing is one more layer inside the worker, a short-lived child per risky call (one plate fill, one sewing, one `BRepCheck`), spawned rather than forked, that receives its input as a BRep string, writes its result to a temp file, and is joined with the stage deadline and then killed. The exit code tells the story: a negative value is the signal that killed it ([Python docs, multiprocessing](https://docs.python.org/3/library/multiprocessing.html)), so −9 after an RSS poll means "that face stays a facet" and the worker continues. The cost is the roughly one second and 330 MB an OCP import takes per child (the figure is from earlier sandbox profiling, not independently verified here), which argues for a persistent helper per stage that is recycled when its RSS crosses the threshold. The earlier research set the memory floor at two OCP-bearing processes plus the parent on an 8 GB machine; a per-call child is a third, which is why it should replace one of the two fit-pool workers on Potato rather than add to them.

The time budget now in the repo is a soft ceiling and should be described as one. `perf.settings` gives Potato `budget_s=9000` (2.5 h) and 20 plate fills, Moderate and Extreme `budget_s=2400` (40 min) and 60–120 fills; the refine loop checks elapsed time against `budget_s` at the top of each pass, stops refining, and allows up to two more settling passes before everything still outside tolerance becomes facets (`app/perf.py`, `app/stl2step_core.py`). What it does not bound is the pass already in flight when the clock runs out (a single 7.7 s fill was observed on the subdivided knob, and a fill on a dense organic mesh could be far longer), the assemble and mend cycles after the loop, or the final STEP write and verification. Splitting the 45 minutes honestly therefore looks like this: the fixed order is cheap exact stages, then checkpoint, then freeform; the freeform stage gets `ceiling − elapsed − reserve`, where the reserve covers the STEP write (16.5 s at 62k faces, far less at a few thousand), one whole-shape check and the deviation measurement, and two minutes is a safe reserve on the i5; inside the freeform stage, process candidates largest-area-first and re-estimate the per-patch cost from the first few timings, since no published runtime model for plate fitting or sewing versus size exists and the app's own per-stage profile is the only calibration available. The 40-minute budget leaves five minutes of reserve, which is enough on every mesh in the test set but is not a guarantee on a mesh nobody has seen; the per-call child is what would make it one. On the i3 the same algorithm simply runs to the 2.5 h ceiling or the user's cancel, and because the checkpoint exists, the extra hours cost electricity and nothing else.

## The face cache is correct and modest; the strip rule is right and marginal

Two prototypes were measured in the sandbox against a read-only copy of the repo, with timing noise of about ±1 s between identical runs. The first attacks the retry loop that the earlier profiling identified as half of a CAD-like part's run time. When any face fails the geometry check, `assemble` reruns `prepare` and `build_all` over every region, up to eight times, and `demote` does the same again for `mend` and for the STEP round-trip check; only the NURBS fits were cached across cycles, while `plane_face`, `prim_face`, both `_face_ok` checks, the swept-primitive `BRepCheck_Analyzer` and `_near_mesh` were redone for every region every cycle. Keying the built-and-checked face under (region type, the region's triangle bytes, and the exact state of its boundary chains: vertex lists, kind, whether smoothed points exist, and the types of the regions on each side) lets `build_all` skip **40–75 % of regions in every cycle after the first**: on the knob, cycle 2 rebuilt 29 and skipped 26, cycle 3 rebuilt 15 and skipped 39; on the tray 11 rebuilt and 102 skipped; on the vase 29 rebuilt and 80 skipped (resA.jsonl (sandbox output, not kept with the repository)). The one thing that made it correct is the deep copy. Sewing and `ShapeFix` mutate the faces they are given in place, and reusing the already-sewn face object changed the knob's maximum deviation from 0.129 mm to 0.317 mm with the same 1,103 faces; storing and returning `BRepBuilderAPI_Copy` copies made the result identical to 16 digits (max deviation 0.1293030564773599 in both runs, volume agreeing to 1e-8 relative). A verification mode that rebuilt every cache hit anyway and compared verdicts found 0 mismatches in 80 hits on the vase.

The honest size of the win is small. Whole-run savings were knob 16.5 → 14.4 s and tray 12.3 → 11.4 s, with plate, knob_sub4 and the vase inside the noise or confounded (the vase's NURBS stage is not run-to-run deterministic even in the released code: three runs gave 7/25, 6/26 and 6/26 NURBS/facet counts with two different max deviations). Across whole runs the cache removed 17–34 % of all `BRepCheck_Analyzer` constructions (knob 1,874 → 1,548, vase 3,248 → 2,146) and 17–39 % of `_face_ok` calls, but the per-phase timing shows why that is not more seconds: on the knob, the per-region work inside `build_all` is about 2.5 s of a 7–8 s assemble/mend phase, and the rest is whole-shape operations that the cache does not touch, `_shells_to_solid` at 2.1–2.2 s over three calls, `_sealed` at 1.9–2.3 s over nine calls, `_safe_unify` at 1.7–1.9 s. The next lever is there: memoise `_sealed` per shape object (the same shape is checked at `mend()` entry, `mend()` return and two guards) and avoid re-sewing unchanged shells. The cache shipped as about 25 lines in `build_all` with `_fresh_copy` at store and hit time (`app/stl2step_core.py`), and it is on for every engine because the output is identical. It was not tested with the Staged engine's `rough.fix_outlines` and `rough.trimmed_face`, which read Builder state and should be checked for purity before anyone trusts the cache on that path.

The second prototype fixes the subdivided-knob regression that the earlier research diagnosed as a density-dependent width estimate. The released rule measures a candidate flat's width as 3.46 times the square root of the smallest covariance eigenvalue over all in-plane vertices and rejects it as a facet row when that is below `0.04·diag` and more than half its border is smooth. On the knob's 1.31 mm-wide flats that estimate is 2.61 mm with four vertices and 1.96 mm after one midpoint subdivision, against a threshold of 2.04 mm, so subdivision drops 44 of 45 flats. The short side of the minimum-area rotated rectangle of the region's boundary vertices (shapely's `minimum_rotated_rectangle` ([Shapely docs](https://shapely.readthedocs.io/en/stable/reference/shapely.minimum_rotated_rectangle.html))) gives **1.31 mm on knob_plain, knob_mid1 and knob_sub4 alike**, 1.74 and 1.22 mm on the shaft's facet rows where the covariance said 3.48 and 2.44, and 8.00 mm on the plate's 8 mm walls where the covariance said 15.98 (resB.json (sandbox output, not kept with the repository)). The uncomfortable finding is that the true width, 1.31 mm, is below the released threshold: the released rule only keeps the knob's flats by over-estimating their width, and a better width with the same threshold loses them on every knob variant. No threshold expressed as a fraction of the diagonal can keep a 1.31 mm flat (2.6 % of the diagonal) while rejecting the tray's 0.71 mm fillet strips (0.7 %) and still mean anything across part sizes.

The replacement is a sagitta test, which is the density- and size-independent form of the same intent. A strip of width w whose smooth borders turn through a mean angle θ is a chord of a surface with radius w/θ, and its sag against that surface is w·θ/8; if that sag is below half the tolerance, a curved fit would absorb the strip, so it is a facet row, and otherwise it is a real flat. Measured sag over tolerance: knob flats 0.82, shaft rows 1.01 and 0.71, plate walls 1.25, tray fillet strips 0.18–0.27, big-fillet strips 0.21–0.31, bracket fillets 0.03–0.09, vase patches 0.13–0.23. With a cutoff of 0.5, every K from 0.3 to 0.7 keeps 45 flats on all three knob variants and reproduces the released plane counts on every other mesh (shaft_coarse 124, shaft_fine 154, bigfillet 20, bracketf 14, bracket 6, plate 15, tray 95, vase 3, blob 0). The one case is the full conversion of knob_sub4 in Classic with the new rule: 66 s and 1,011 faces instead of 88 s and a 2,234-face faceted fallback, but with a **maximum deviation of 0.61 mm** against 0.129 mm on knob_plain, seven facet regions and six geometry-check failures, because the NURBS and fillet patches between the flats on the denser mesh fit worse (resB.json (sandbox output, not kept with the repository)). The rule changes which route the part takes; it does not make the route good. That is why the repo gates it: in `find_planes` the OBB width and sagitta apply only when `prm.ladder` is set, as an AND with the released width test so it is strictly looser than before, and Classic keeps its released behaviour (`app/stl2step_core.py`). The changelog's Ladder figure for the same mesh, 20 s and 967 faces at 0.07 mm, is the app's own sandbox claim (`CHANGELOG.md`) and was not part of this measurement set. The remaining exposure is a coarse-cylinder part with soft-ended facet rows whose sag over tolerance falls in the 0.34–0.82 band where the released rule and the sagitta rule disagree; no mesh in the corpus has one, so that risk is unmeasured.

## The i5 is about twice the sandbox; the i3 is about the sandbox

PassMark rates the i5-1135G7 at CPU Mark 9,290 and single-thread 2,593 against the i3-7100U's 2,720 and 1,365, ratios of **3.42× multi-threaded and 1.90× single-threaded**; the i5 is 4 cores / 8 threads at 2.4 GHz base and 4.2 GHz turbo with 8 MB L3, the i3 is 2 cores / 4 threads at 2.4 GHz with no turbo and 3 MB L3 ([cpubenchmark.net](https://www.cpubenchmark.net/compare/Intel-i5-1135G7-vs-Intel-i3-7100U/3830vs2879)). Geekbench 6 agrees at 2.01× single and 3.16× multi ([cpu-monkey](https://www.cpu-monkey.com/en/compare_cpu-intel_core_i5_1135g7-vs-intel_core_i3_7100u)). The catch is thermal and power limits. Notebookcheck lists the i5-1135G7's configurable TDP as 12–28 W, its all-core boost at 3.8 GHz, and a Cinebench R23 multi-core spread of 2,786–6,136 across laptops carrying the same chip, with the best "about 60% faster in CPU-bound workloads than the slowest" ([Notebookcheck, i5-1135G7](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Intel-Core-i5-1135G7-Processor-Benchmarks-and-Specs.467754.0.html)); in a Dell Latitude 3520 the all-core clock "initially processed with 3.3 to 3.5 GHz, then the clock drops to 3.1 to 3.2 GHz" under a sustained loop, with PL1 anywhere from 14.75 W to 30 W depending on the power profile ([Notebookcheck, Latitude 3520](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Dell-Latitude-3520-in-review-Core-i5-office-laptop-delivers-good-runtimes.697287.0.html)). The i3 has no turbo, so it has no burst-versus-sustained gap at all.

For planning, the sustained numbers are the ones to use: the i5 is roughly 2.5–3× the i3 on multi-threaded work and 1.7–1.9× on single-threaded work, and since sewing, plate filling, `ShapeFix`, `UnifySameDomain` and the STEP writer are all single-threaded, a whole conversion scales closer to the single-thread ratio. The sandbox is a 2-vCPU Xeon container (reported at 2.1 GHz in the earlier sessions and 2.8 GHz in this one, with `OMP_NUM_THREADS=1`), which has no published benchmark for its exact SKU; the working assumption, consistent with the earlier reports' 1.0–1.6× estimate, is that **the i3 takes about the sandbox single-thread time, and the i5 about half of it**, which is the factor the repo's `ladder.estimate` prints ("on a 2-core laptop (an 11th-gen i5 about half that)") (`app/ladder.py`). Applied to the worst sandbox runs, the 24k-triangle vase in Classic at 258 s becomes roughly two minutes on the i5 and four to five on the i3, and the subdivided knob at 88 s becomes under a minute on the i5; nothing in the test set comes near 45 minutes on either machine. A job that genuinely takes 45 minutes on the i5 would take 1.5–2 hours on the i3, which is inside the 2.5 h Potato ceiling, so the two budgets are consistent with each other. The honest caveat is that none of this was measured on either laptop; the app should time a fixed fixture on first launch and store the factor, which is a few lines and would replace this entire section with a number.

Memory, not CPU, is the binding constraint on the 4 GB i3. Windows pages into a system-managed pagefile and Linux thrashes before it kills, so the 60 %-of-available soft cap on the risky calls has to fire before any speed consideration applies, and on that machine the plate filler should probably be off by default rather than skipped at 75 % of total RAM.

## Honesty ledger

**Published.** The anytime-algorithm framing (Barath & Matas 2019); `os.replace` atomicity and `os.fsync` (Python docs); STEP write and read-back cost at 62k faces and the 20,000-face advice (mesh2step); the Fusion 360 and FreeCAD large-STEP failures (Autodesk Community; FreeCAD #18735); `BRepTools`, `STEPControl_Writer`, `GeomPlate_BuildPlateSurface`, `BRepBuilderAPI_Sewing`, `IMeshTools_Parameters`, `BRepCheck_Analyzer` and `Message_ProgressIndicator` signatures (OCCT reference manual); progress coverage by version (OCCT 7.5.0 and 7.6.0 release notes; Gavrilov's write-up); the uncovered code paths and segfault warning (FreeCAD PR #19763; Stanley 2025; Cadly #5); Linux overcommit modes and desktop freeze behaviour (kernel.org; Arch forum; bazzite #5426); Windows commit limit and pagefile growth (Microsoft TechNet wiki); Job Object memory limits (Microsoft Learn); the failure of RLIMIT caps for NumPy workloads (numpy #26551; Python resource docs); `multiprocessing` termination semantics (Python docs); plate-fill crash reports and settled parameters (CadQuery #229); `UnifySameDomain` cost and hangs (pythonocc-core #1477; XbimGeometry #483); CPU benchmarks and sustained-clock behaviour (PassMark; cpu-monkey; Notebookcheck). Not verified: the STEP user guide's mapping of a mixed solid-plus-shell compound; whether OCP exposes a trampoline for `Message_ProgressIndicator`; whether pybind11's `bad_alloc` mapping survives OCCT's own out-of-memory paths; any published memory figure for any OCCT algorithm; the i3-7020U variant (treated as about 4 % slower than the 7100U); macOS memory-pressure behaviour.

**Sandbox, not an i3.** Every face-cache and strip-rule number (region counts per cycle, the 0.129 versus 0.317 mm copy finding, the 16-digit identity, the per-phase timings, the OBB widths, the sag/tol table, the K sweep, the 66 s / 1,011 faces / 0.61 mm knob_sub4 conversion) comes from a 2-vCPU Xeon container running the Classic engine on a scratch copy of `stl2step_core.py`, with ±1 s run-to-run noise, over twelve test meshes, and with the vase baseline itself non-deterministic. The Staged and Ladder engines were not run in this measurement set; the changelog's 20 s / 967 faces / 0.07 mm Ladder figure is the app's own claim from the same class of machine. The repo features in the ranked table were read from the source as it stands today, not benchmarked after being merged. Nothing was run on the i3, the i5, Windows or macOS.

**Inference.** The checkpoint-then-overwrite pipeline design; the claim that Linux kills and Windows grinds is sourced, but what either does to this app's worker specifically is reasoned, not observed; the per-call child-process architecture, its 60 %-of-available threshold, the 250 ms poll and the persistent-helper recycling; the budget split (fixed order, `ceiling − elapsed − reserve`, two-minute reserve, largest-area-first with online re-estimation); the judgement that `_memory_tight`'s 75 % threshold fires late on 4 GB; the sagitta derivation and the 0.5 constant; the "next lever is `_sealed` memoisation" conclusion; the sandbox-to-laptop scaling factors (i3 ≈ sandbox, i5 ≈ half) and the 1.5–2 h i3 equivalent of a 45-minute i5 job; the ranking itself.

## Conclusion

The question was how to make a converter safe on a machine that cannot be trusted to stay alive, and the answer turned out to be less about defensive code than about the order of operations: do the cheap exact work, write the file, and only then do the work that can kill you. Once that order exists, the elaborate machinery of memory limits and cancellation tokens becomes a matter of how much CPU is wasted before the inevitable kill, not whether the user gets a file. That reframing also exposes what the current budget really is. A clock checked at the top of each pass is a promise to stop asking for more work; it is not a promise that the work already requested will stop, and on OpenCASCADE calls that do not poll, no clock in Python can be. The repo is now honest about that in its log lines, and the next step is a process boundary around each slow call, which is the one addition that converts the Moderate profile's 40 minutes from an intention into a ceiling.

The strip-rule result is the more interesting lesson for the fitting side. The released code kept the knob's flats by accident, through a width estimate that happened to be wrong in the right direction, and a correct measurement made the ambiguity visible: a 48-gon of 1.3 mm flats sits 82 % of a tolerance from a cylinder, so whether it is 48 planes or one round face is a decision the app has to own, not one a threshold can hide. Gating the fix to the Ladder engine is the right call for now, and the 0.6 mm case it produced in Classic is a reminder that a better segmentation only pays off when the fitting behind it is good enough to use what it finds.
